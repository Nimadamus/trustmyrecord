A .916 Team Save Percentage Against an .893

The best crease in the league visits an average one

Minnesota arrives at SAP Center at 17-3-5 with the league's best team save percentage, a .916 mark that ranks 1st of 32. San Jose sits at 11-12-2 with a team save percentage of .893, which is 14th of 32 and squarely in the middle of the pack. That's the whole premise of this game: one crease that has been a wall for every skater in front of it, and one that has been merely fine. Fine doesn't cover much when the other side is shooting into a wall.

Jesper Wallstedt is listed to start for the Wild, and Yaroslav Askarov is listed to start for the Sharks. We aren't quoting either goalie's own numbers; the .916 and the .893 are team save percentages, built by every netminder and every defender each club has dressed. That distinction matters. A team save percentage that leads the league across 25 games says as much about structure as it does about the man in the mask.

The edge

One crease has been a wall all season. The other has been fine, and fine rarely holds against a wall.

Where the Saves Show Up on the Scoreboard

Save percentage becomes goals allowed, and the table agrees

Save percentage reaches the scoreboard as goals against, and the scoreboard backs it up. The Wild allow 2.24 goals a game, 1st of 32, while the Sharks allow 3.56, 24th of 32. San Jose's .893 team save percentage ranks 14th of 32, yet its goals allowed rank sits well below that at 24th, a gap the saves alone don't explain. Minnesota has no such mismatch: its .916 team save percentage and its goals allowed both sit 1st of 32.

What's striking is how the Wild have done it. Neither club has a shutout yet, 0 apiece, both tied 19th of 32, so Minnesota's edge isn't propped up by a few blank sheets. It's the steady, nightly kind of goal prevention, the sort that holds up over a 17-3-5 start rather than spiking and fading. San Jose at 11-12-2 hasn't found that floor.

The edge

No shutouts, no fluke nights. Minnesota's goal prevention is the steady, every game kind.

Margins Built in the Crease

Minnesota's differential leans on the goals it doesn't concede

Minnesota's +34 goal differential, +1.36 a game and 2nd of 32, is what a top team save percentage produces when it's paired with a real attack. The Wild score 3.6 goals a game, 7th of 32, on 12.4 percent shooting, 9th of 32. Good numbers, but not the best in the league. The part of the margin that is the best in the league is the 2.24 goals allowed a game, 1st of 32.

San Jose is the mirror image. The Sharks are -20 in goal differential, -0.8 a game and 29th of 32, and they shoot just 9.6 percent, also 29th of 32. Run a 9.6 percent finishing rate into a .916 team save percentage, the league's best, and the arithmetic turns unkind fast. There's little in the Sharks' scoring line to suggest they can manufacture the extra goals that kind of crease demands.

The edge

Weak finishing into the league's best crease is the matchup San Jose least wanted.

The Sharks' Attack Meets the Wall

A below average offence against the stingiest unit in the league

The Sharks score 2.76 goals a game, 27th of 32, and they run into a defence allowing 2.24, 1st of 32. That's a below average attack facing the stingiest unit in the league, backed by a .916 team save percentage. SAP Center hasn't been much of a fortress for San Jose, which is 6-6-1 at home. Minnesota, meanwhile, is 11-3-3 on the road and 8-0-2 over its last ten.

Form doesn't rescue the home side either. The Sharks are 4-5-1 over their last ten and 11-12-2 overall, the record of a club that needs its goaltending to steal nights. A .893 team save percentage, 14th of 32, steals some nights, just not many. Against a team that hasn't lost in regulation over its last ten, San Jose's crease has to be the best player on the ice, and its season says that hasn't been the norm.

The edge

San Jose needs its crease to steal this one, and its season says that's the exception.

Special Teams Put the Crease Under Pressure

The power play is where a save percentage gets tested hardest

Special teams lean the same way. Minnesota converts 25.6 percent on the power play, 8th of 32, and San Jose kills only 73.9 percent of penalties, 22nd of 32. A penalty kill is where a goaltender earns his keep, and a team save percentage of .893, 14th of 32, leaves the Sharks less margin there than they'd like. Every Wild power play is a direct test of the weaker crease.

Going the other way, the Sharks' 21.3 percent power play ranks 18th of 32, against a Wild kill at 79.7 percent, 13th of 32. That's the one area where Minnesota is ordinary rather than elite, and it's San Jose's likeliest route to goals. Even then, a man advantage still has to beat the league's best team save percentage, .916, to count. The Wild's ordinary kill is protected by an extraordinary crease.

The edge

Minnesota's kill is ordinary. The goaltending behind it isn't, and that covers the gap.
