The league's stingiest defence walks into a building that scores

Boston's 3.68 goals a game this season against Minnesota's 2.19 allowed

Boston is scoring 3.68 goals a game this season, 6th of 32 in the league. Minnesota is allowing 2.19, which ranks 1st of 32. That's the whole game in a sentence: a home attack that has been among the league's best running into the defence nobody has solved consistently. The Bruins are 13-5-1 and the Wild are 14-3-4, so neither number comes from a club that's been losing.

The reason to lean toward the attack is where the game is played and how Boston has been getting its goals. The Bruins are 8-2-0 at TD Garden this season, and their scoring rate hasn't needed much help from the power play, which we'll get to. Minnesota's 2.19 is a real number, but it's a 21 game number, and it's about to face a team whose 13.4 shooting percentage ranks 3rd of 32.

Boston's scoring this season doesn't run through the power play

A 13.4 shooting percentage is doing the work an 18 percent power play isn't

Boston's shooting percentage is 13.4 this season, 3rd of 32. That's the engine. The power play isn't: it's clicking at 18 percent, which sits 27th of 32. So a club scoring 3.68 goals a game is doing it with a man advantage unit that ranks near the bottom of the league, and that tells you the production doesn't depend on drawing penalties.

There's a generous read of that and a sceptical one. The generous read is that Boston's attack travels well into a tight game, because it doesn't lean on the least predictable part of a hockey night. The sceptical read is that a 13.4 shooting percentage over 19 games is the kind of figure that tends to cool, and Minnesota's penalty kill, 79.7 percent and 11th of 32 this season, isn't going to hand the Bruins something their power play hasn't been taking anyway. Both reads can be true at once.

Why Minnesota's 2.19 has held up this season

A .915 team save percentage and not a single shutout

Minnesota's team save percentage is .915 this season, 2nd of 32, and that's the spine of the 2.19 goals allowed a game. What's curious is the shutout column: the Wild have 0, which ranks 18th of 32. They haven't been blanking anybody. They've been holding opponents to a low number night after night, which is arguably a sturdier way to lead the league than a few spectacular outliers.

Jesper Wallstedt is listed to start for Minnesota, and Jeremy Swayman is listed to start for Boston. Those are listings, not guarantees, and the save percentages here belong to each whole team rather than to either goalie. Still, Boston's side of this matters to the central question, because the Bruins' own team save percentage is .917 this season, 1st of 32, with 2 shutouts, 2nd of 32. If the Wild defence does slow Boston down, the Bruins have the goaltending numbers to win a low scoring game anyway.

The wall

Minnesota leads the league in goals allowed with a .915 team save percentage and 0 shutouts. It's consistency, not outliers.

Flip the ice and Minnesota's edge is on the power play

The Wild's 3.33 goals a game this season against Boston's 2.42 allowed

Turn the matchup around and the picture is similar but a shade softer. Minnesota scores 3.33 goals a game this season, 10th of 32, against a Boston defence allowing 2.42, 3rd of 32. Both attacks sit well above what the opposing defence usually concedes. But Boston's attack ranks higher than Minnesota's, and the distance between the defences is small.

Where the Wild do hold a clear edge is the power play: 27.4 percent this season, 6th of 32, against a Boston penalty kill at 78.5 percent, 14th of 32. Minnesota's shooting percentage is 11.4, 17th of 32, so its scoring leans more on the man advantage than Boston's does. That makes discipline the Bruins' easiest lever. Stay out of the box and the Wild attack loses the part of its game that ranks best.

The counter

Minnesota's power play runs at 27.4 percent this season, 6th of 32. Boston's penalty kill is 14th of 32 at 78.5.

A thin edge, and it belongs to the home attack

Form favours Minnesota, venue and per game margin favour Boston

Nobody should pretend this is lopsided. The Wild are 8-0-2 in their last ten and 9-3-2 on the road this season; the Bruins are 6-3-1 in their last ten. Minnesota has 32 standings points from 21 games and Boston has 27 from 19. Both clubs sit at a +24 goal differential.

Per game, though, Boston's differential is +1.26, 2nd of 32, against Minnesota's +1.14, 4th of 32, and the Bruins are 8-2-0 at home this season. That's a thin margin, and it's the right size for the conclusion. The home attack has the edge because it's the higher ranked attack in this game, it's playing in its own building, and it doesn't need a power play to score. It's an edge, not a forecast of a rout.

The margin
