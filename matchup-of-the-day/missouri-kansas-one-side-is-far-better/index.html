Missouri's +153 Point Differential Last Season Against a +16

Two 2025 scoring margins that are nowhere near each other

Missouri went 8-4 last season with a +153 point differential. Kansas went 5-7 with a +16 point differential across the same twelve 2025 games. Records can lie about quality, and often do, but a scoring margin that far apart over a full season usually isn't lying. This is the number the matchup turns on, and everything else here is either feeding it or threatening it.

Look at how each side got there last season. Missouri scored 32.2 points a game in 2025 and gave up 19.4, so the Tigers were winning the scoreboard at both ends. Kansas scored 28.1 and allowed 26.8, which describes a team that had to keep winning close games just to stay near even. The Jayhawks' +16 point differential in 2025 is what that kind of season looks like once you add it up.

The edge

A +153 point differential last season against a +16 is the whole argument; the rest is detail.

The 2025 Defense Is Where Missouri's Margin Actually Came From

Scoring looked similar last season; prevention did not

The offenses weren't that far apart last season. Missouri put up 32.2 points a game in 2025 and Kansas put up 28.1, a difference you could talk yourself out of caring about. The separation sits on the other side of the ball: 19.4 points allowed for Missouri against 26.8 for Kansas over those 2025 games. That is where a +153 point differential and a +16 point differential come from.

Pressure is part of the story. Missouri got to the quarterback 35 times last season, 14th among 136 teams, while Kansas managed 25 sacks, 63rd in 2025. Sacks aren't a defense on their own, but they line up with the points allowed gap rather than cutting against it. When two numbers measuring different things point the same direction, the margin is usually real.

The edge

Last season the offenses looked alike; 19.4 points allowed against 26.8 is what separated them.

Missouri Ran for 234.1 Yards a Game Last Season

How the Tigers controlled 2025 games before the scoreboard showed it

Margins like Missouri's are usually built on something repeatable, and last season it was the run. The Tigers averaged 234.1 rushing yards a game in 2025, 8th of 136 teams, at 5.34 yards a carry, 16th. Kansas ran for 168.3 a game last season, 60th, at 4.7 a carry, 43rd. One of those is an identity; the other is a complement.

The passing numbers cut the other way and deserve honesty. Missouri threw for 195.3 yards a game in 2025, 103rd of 136, at 6.38 yards an attempt, 89th. Kansas averaged 7.33 yards per pass attempt last season, 41st, the one place the Jayhawks clearly owned the better per play number. If Kansas turns that into finished drives at home, the 2025 margin gap predicts less than it appears to.

The edge

Last season Missouri ran for 234.1 yards a game at 5.34 a carry; that is how a scoring margin gets built on purpose.

Missouri Converted 46.1 Percent of Third Downs Last Season

Drives that continued in 2025 against drives that ended

Third down is where a run game becomes points or becomes a punt. Missouri converted 46.1 percent of third downs last season, 28th of 136 teams, while Kansas converted 38.5 percent, 83rd in 2025. Across a season that is a pile of extra snaps for one side and a pile of extra punts for the other. It shows up in the first down counts too: 23.8 a game for Missouri last season, 14th, against 20.7 for Kansas, 64th.

Total yardage tells a milder version of the same story. Missouri gained 429.3 yards a game in 2025, 27th of 136, and Kansas gained 390.5, 67th. The yardage gap is narrower than the points gap, which is the honest caveat: Kansas moved the ball last season without finishing at Missouri's rate. Finishing is the skill that eventually shows up in a point differential.

The edge

46.1 percent on third down last season against 38.5 percent is the difference between drives and punts.

Missouri Built a +153 Point Differential Last Season Despite a -5 Turnover Differential

Giveaways and a road record that argue against the 2025 gap

Here is the crack in the argument. Missouri carried a -5 turnover differential last season, 33rd of 136 teams, with 11 takeaways, 109th, against 16 giveaways, 76th. Building a +153 point differential in 2025 while losing the turnover battle is impressive and also a little fragile. Kansas sat at a -3 turnover differential last season, 77th, with 10 takeaways, 113th, and 13 giveaways, 36th, so neither defense was taking the ball away much.

Venue and discipline matter too. Missouri went 2-2 on the road last season while Kansas went 4-3 at home in 2025, the narrowest split on this page. Penalties don't help the Tigers either: 625 penalty yards last season against 477 for Kansas. None of that erases the scoring margin gap, but it is the exact shape a Kansas upset would take.

The edge

A +153 point differential last season built on top of a -5 turnover differential is real, and it is not bulletproof.

Both Teams Are 1-0 in 2026, Only One Has Last Season's Margin Behind It

One game played, and twelve 2025 games of evidence

Both teams are 1-0 in 2026, which tells you close to nothing yet. What we do have is a full season on each: Missouri 8-4 last season with a +153 point differential, Kansas 5-7 with a +16 point differential. Until the 2026 sample grows, last season's margin is the best available read on which roster is better. That isn't a perfect answer, it's the least bad one.

The case for Kansas is location and variance. The Jayhawks went 4-3 at home last season while Missouri went 2-2 on the road in 2025, and one strange Saturday can swallow a year's worth of margin. But backing that case means betting against the largest, cleanest gap available. Missouri's +153 point differential last season against Kansas's +16 is the thing you have to explain away, and a home crowd doesn't quite do it.

The edge

Two 1-0 teams in 2026, and only one of them arrives with a +153 point differential from last season.
