Where this one actually gets decided

ESPN Data

One of these offensive lines kept its quarterback clean all year. The other one really didn’t.

Here are the two numbers that made this the Game File, and they’re not close. Colorado gave up 38 sacks last season. Georgia Tech gave up 9. That’s not two teams a little apart on the same scale. That’s two different experiences of playing football, and almost everything else on this page is downstream of it.

The first thing you should want to know is whether it’s a volume illusion, and it isn’t. Colorado ran 826 offensive plays and Georgia Tech ran 798, so both lines were asked to do roughly the same job the same number of times. Per snap that comes out at 4.6 percent for Colorado and 1.1 percent for Georgia Tech. A quarterback absorbing four times the punishment for the same number of chances is about the most reliable way there is to lose a game you were otherwise in.

Now flip it and look at the other side of the ball, because that’s where it gets uncomfortable for Colorado. Georgia Tech got home for 26 sacks and 135 yards last season. Colorado managed 13. So this is the line that couldn’t protect meeting the front that finished twice as often as anyone Colorado sent. Tackles for loss are basically even at 62 to 60, which tells you the Buffaloes can get into a backfield. Finishing the play once they are in there is the part they never solved.

Why a sack costs more than it looksTMR Research

A sack isn’t just lost yardage. It kills a drive, it burns clock you wanted back, and it turns a manageable second down into a punt. Teams that lead their conference in sacks allowed rarely lose because of any single play. They lose because they spend the whole season behind the sticks and never get a normal down to work with.

None of this is 2026 evidence, and I want to be straight about that. Nobody has a snap of tape on either of these teams this year. What we do have is a full season of both, most of the same staffs in the rooms that matter, and in Colorado’s case a problem that was loud from the opening Saturday to the last one.

Colorado hasn’t won away from home in a very long time

ESPN Data

Not one road win all of last season, and the drop from the year before is the part that should worry you.

Colorado went 0-5 on the road in 2025 and most of it wasn’t close. Utah beat them 53-7. At home they were a perfectly respectable 3-4. Away from Folsom Field they didn’t win once, and that’s the profile of a team that needs its own crowd and its own altitude to be itself.

You have to go back to the 2024 season to find their last road win, a 41-27 win at Texas Tech in November of that year. That matters because 2024 Colorado was a genuinely good team. They finished 9-3 and went 4-2 away from Boulder. A year later the same programme was 3-9 with an 0-5 road record. Teams don’t usually fall that far in twelve months without something structural breaking, and the thing that broke shows up in the sack column.

Georgia Tech moved the other way over the same two years, which is worth putting side by side. They were 7-5 in 2024 and won one road game all season. In 2025 they were 9-3 and went 4-1 away from home, 6-2 in the ACC, and Colorado finished 1-8 in the Big 12. One of these programmes has been trending up for two years. The other has been trending down for one, and hard.

The one I keep coming back to isn’t a road loss at all. Arizona went into Boulder in November and won 52-17. You can forgive a team for getting beaten in Salt Lake City. Getting beaten by five touchdowns in your own stadium, in front of your own people, is a different kind of evidence, and it’s the last complete look anybody has at this defence.

So no, none of that guarantees anything about a Thursday night in September. Rosters turn over and Colorado’s had a full off season to fix what broke. But the board isn’t pricing hope. It’s pricing what got measured, and what got measured was a team that scored 251 points in 12 games while giving up 366.

They already played this exact game once

ESPN Data

Georgia Tech went to Boulder to open last season and won it. Now it comes back to Atlanta.

These two opened last season against each other at Folsom Field, and Georgia Tech won 27-20. Think about what that actually was: a road win at altitude in week one, against this same programme, in front of a Colorado crowd that had spent all summer expecting more. Now the fixture swings back to Atlanta and the Yellow Jackets get the easier half of the deal.

One game is one game and I wouldn’t build a whole case on it. What it does is remove an excuse. Georgia Tech has already proved it can travel into this matchup and win. Colorado hasn’t proved it can win this matchup at home, let alone on the road, and the version of Colorado that lost that opener still had an experienced quarterback taking the snaps who has since moved on after 1,414 passing yards.

Georgia Tech runs it, and Colorado could not stop anybody running it

ESPN Data

Two hundred yards a game on the ground against a front that gave up three and a half a carry.

Georgia Tech averaged 203.0 rushing yards a game at 5.6 a carry and scored 30 rushing touchdowns. Colorado averaged 125.6 at 3.5 a carry with 15 on the ground. That is the second structural gap on this card, and it is the one that decides what the fourth quarter looks like when somebody is trying to close a game out.

The efficiency numbers back it up rather than argue with it. Georgia Tech converted 47.18 percent on third down and 38.46 percent on fourth, and picked up 283 first downs. Colorado was at 38.42 percent on third, 33.33 percent on fourth, and 208 first downs across the same 12 games. Call that six extra first downs a week. Over a night that is a possession and a half of free football, which is roughly what a touchdown costs.

Here’s the part that makes me comfortable with a brand new quarterback. Georgia Tech returns Malachi Hosley, who ran for 634 yards at 7.3 a carry with 7 touchdowns on only 87 carries last season, and they have added Justice Haynes, who ran for 857 yards at 7.1 a carry with 10 touchdowns in seven games at Michigan. Two backs who both cleared seven yards a carry, behind the line that allowed 9 sacks. Whoever takes the first snap on Thursday doesn’t have to be a hero. He has to hand it off and not lose the ball.

Colorado brings back Micah Welch, who led them with 384 yards on 96 carries and 4 touchdowns. Read that again. Their leading rusher averaged four yards a carry on a team that finished 3-9. That’s the whole problem said politely: there was never a back who could take the weight off the passing game, so every second and long turned into a throw, and every throw was another chance for that sack number to climb.

One team is starting somebody new because it has to. The other one wanted to.

ESPN Data

This is the strongest argument against the pick, so let us make it properly.

Georgia Tech is replacing the single most productive player in its offence, and it isn’t close. Haynes King threw for 2,697 yards and 12 touchdowns last season and ran for 922 more with 15 rushing scores. He accounted for more of that team than anybody else on it, and he’s not on the 2026 roster. Neither is Aaron Philo, who was the only other quarterback to throw a touchdown pass for them.

What’s left is genuinely thin on live reps. The most experienced arm Georgia Tech returns is Graham Knowles, and his entire college passing career at this point is one attempt, one completion, 84 yards, in one game. That’s a lovely stat and a terrifying one, and it’s the honest reason this is a moneyline lean rather than anything bigger.

Colorado is in a much better spot here and they know it. Julian Lewis played four games as a freshman last season and went 52 of 94 for 589 yards, 4 touchdowns and zero interceptions. Zero. He has had a full off season as the presumed starter instead of the emergency option, and he throws to Joseph Williams, who caught 37 passes for 489 yards and 4 scores.

And yet. Dig one level into that Lewis line and you find the thing that keeps pulling me back to Georgia Tech. In those four games he was sacked 12 times. Three a game, for a freshman, in a season where the team gave up 38 in total. He wasn’t bad. He was under water, and nothing about the shape of that offence has obviously changed.

So the question isn’t which team has the better quarterback situation on paper, because Colorado does. The question is which quarterback is being asked to do something possible. Georgia Tech’s new starter can hand off, take his completions and let the defence and the kicker win a low event football game. Colorado’s returning starter has to go make one happen behind that line, against a front that got to the quarterback 26 times.

There is a continuity argument on defence too, and it points the same way. Georgia Tech returns both of its leading tacklers from last season, Kyle Efford with 72 and E.J. Lightsey with 70. The unit that has to hold an unfamiliar offence up is the one that has changed the least.

The phase nobody previews, which is usually the one that pays

ESPN Data

Kicking, punting and field position, in a game the market thinks lands inside a touchdown.

Georgia Tech made 25 of 28 field goals last season, 89.3 percent, with a long of 55 and 117 points off the foot. Colorado made 9 of 12, or 75.0 percent, and attempted less than half as many. In a game the board expects to be decided by about a touchdown, a kicker who converts nine out of ten is worth more than most of the skill players on the field.

The part I like most is that Aidan Birr, the kicker who did all of that, is on the 2026 roster. That’s not a small thing when a team is breaking in a new quarterback. It means every drive that stalls short of the end zone is still worth points, which is exactly the safety net a first time starter needs.

Punting says the same thing more quietly. Georgia Tech averaged 43.1 net yards a punt against 37.2 for Colorado. Six net yards a punt, over the five or six punts a normal college game produces, is another thirty odd yards of field position handed to the home team for nothing at all.

Discipline is close enough to call it even. Georgia Tech gave up 565 penalty yards on 71 flags, Colorado 599 on 68. Neither team is clean. Neither has an edge worth putting in a bet.

One more thing that’s easy to wave away and probably shouldn’t be. Kickoff at Bobby Dodd is forecast mostly sunny and 93 degrees. That’s a September evening in Atlanta, and the visitors have spent August at five thousand feet in Boulder in dry air. Heat doesn’t decide football games on its own. It does decide how deep into a defensive line rotation you have to go by the fourth quarter, and Georgia Tech has the deeper front.

What the market is telling you, and where it has moved

DraftKings

The Georgia Tech price has come in about fifty cents since it opened.

Georgia Tech is -6.5 at -108 with a total of 51.5. The interesting bit is the moneyline. The Yellow Jackets opened -290 and they’re -238 now, while Colorado has gone from +235 to +195. That’s a 52 cent move toward the visitor before anybody has run a play.

In an opener a move like that is usually money arriving on the side with the story, and Colorado has the story. Young quarterback back, full off season, a number that looked generous the second it went up. I wouldn’t call that sharp. It is what shows up when a famous team is a live dog.

What it does do is improve the price we care about. At -238 you need to win 70.4 percent of the time to break even. At the opening -290 you needed close to three in four. So anybody who thinks Georgia Tech wins this game seven times out of ten is being asked to pay less for that opinion than they were a week ago, which is the only reason this is worth writing up at all.

Two other prices matter to how you take the side. The spread is -6.5, so a Georgia Tech win by exactly six loses on the number and wins on the moneyline, which is a real gap in a game with a kicker this good. The total is 51.5, and a game that stays under it is one where a single score decides everything.

How this pick loses

Written before kickoff rather than explained afterwards.

  • Georgia Tech has to find a quarterback in live fire. The player who carried that offence is gone, and the most experienced replacement has thrown one pass in his career. one attempt, 84 yards
  • Turnovers were already the soft spot. Georgia Tech finished at -6 on turnover differential with only 7 takeaways, while Colorado forced 15. A team that doesn’t take the ball away has no cushion when its own offence gives it up.
  • Openers are the noisiest week of the year. Everything on this page is built on last season, and last season is exactly the sample a September rebuild exists to break.

The turnover point is the serious one, and it’s why this is a moneyline call and not a bigger position. Georgia Tech finished -6 in that column and still won nine games, which is a compliment to everything else on the roster and a warning about the variance underneath it. Colorado forced 15 takeaways and defended 42 passes, more than Georgia Tech’s 35. If this turns into a game decided by two short fields, the visitor is the more likely side to have created them.

And it would be dishonest to skip how Georgia Tech finished. Pittsburgh won 42-28 in Atlanta and Georgia won 16-9, so the last two live looks anybody has at this programme are both losses, one of them at home by two scores. A team that closed on a two game losing run isn’t an untouchable favourite and shouldn’t be priced like one.

Prediction

One call, the reasons behind it, and what would talk me out of it.

Georgia Tech wins this football game. The line of scrimmage isn’t close, the home team returns two backs who both averaged better than seven yards a carry, it returns both leading tacklers and the kicker who made 25 of 28, and the visitor has not won away from home since the 2024 season. A brand new quarterback lowers the ceiling on how well Georgia Tech can play on Thursday. It doesn’t change who’s stronger in the phases that actually decide a one score game.

The number you have to hold your nose at is the price, and I’m not going to pretend otherwise. -238 isn’t a bargain. It is what it costs to take the more complete football team in a spot where the incomplete one has to cross two time zones, come down off altitude into 93 degree heat, and protect a quarterback behind a line that couldn’t do it once last year.
