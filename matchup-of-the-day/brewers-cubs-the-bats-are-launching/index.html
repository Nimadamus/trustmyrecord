The Bats Are Launching

The best home run lineup in the sport, and it is still swinging hot

Start with the number that brought you here: the Cubs have hit 197 home runs, 1st in baseball. That isn't a fluke of one hot month either, because they also sit 1st in OPS at .772 and 1st in runs scored with 751. This is the best lineup in the sport by every counting stat that matters, and its signature is the ball leaving the yard. The Brewers, by contrast, have 130 homers and rank 29th, which tells you how differently these two offenses are built.

Now layer in the form. Chicago has scored 8.6 runs per game across its last ten, and that stretch includes the series opener, a 3-17 game the Cubs turned into a rout. Against right-handed pitching the Cubs post a .757 OPS, which is the split Henderson has to live in. The question isn't whether this lineup can hurt him; it's whether it can hurt him in the one way he has actually been hurt this year.

The edge

197 home runs, 1st in the majors, against a starter whose only real vulnerability has been the ball in the air.

Henderson, Elite Everywhere but Over the Fence

Nobody reaches base against him, so the damage has to come in one swing

Here is the honest problem with the angle: Logan Henderson's line is absurd. He's 9-2 with a 2.48 ERA and a 0.83 WHIP over 76.1 innings, opponents are batting .181 against him, and he has struck out 86 in 14 starts, a rate of 10.14 per nine. He walks 1.65 per nine, so the free base is off the table and every rally has to be earned with the bat. The last five starts haven't shown any slippage either; his ERA in that stretch is 2.23.

So where does the damage come from? Not from traffic, because he holds left-handed hitters to a .573 OPS and right-handers to a .561 OPS, which are numbers you'd expect from a closer, not a starter. When he has been hurt, it has been the ball in the air, the swing that turns a clean inning into a run without any traffic in front of it. That is exactly the profile the Cubs' 197 homers are built to exploit, and Wrigley in early September with the wind blowing out can turn a warning track out into a home run.

The catch

A .181 opponents' average and a 0.83 WHIP, and yet the ball in the air is where Henderson bleeds.

Two Very Different Ways to Build a Contender

Chicago scores more; Milwaukee prevents far more

Milwaukee is 87-53 with a +172 run differential; Chicago is 78-62 at +132. The gap between them is entirely on the run prevention side, because Chicago has actually scored more, 751 runs to 704. The Brewers have allowed 532 runs on a 3.48 staff ERA that ranks 2nd, while the Cubs have given up 619 on a 4.21 ERA that ranks 18th. Offensively it's closer than the home run column suggests: Milwaukee's .740 team OPS ranks 4th and its runs total ranks 3rd, so this is not a one dimensional pitching club.

That framing matters tonight because the Cubs' path to winning runs through their bats, not their arms. Kevin Gausman brings a 4.52 ERA and a 1.27 WHIP over 155.1 innings with an 8-11 record, and his last five starts have gone worse at a 5.14 ERA. Opponents are batting .258 against him, and the Brewers' .756 OPS against right-handers is their better split by a clear margin over the .704 OPS they manage against lefties. Chicago will probably need to score a lot, and the good news for them is that scoring a lot is the thing they do best.

What the Last Three Days Told Us

The Cubs' bats never went quiet this week; their pitching did

Milwaukee leads the season series 6-3 and has outscored Chicago 51-45 across the nine meetings, but the split inside this week is the interesting part. The Cubs won the opener, a 3-17 scoreline that read like a softball box score, then dropped the next two, 9-4 and 9-5, as the Brewers' offense answered. The lineup didn't go quiet in those losses; the pitching just couldn't hold anything. That's the Cubs' season in miniature: a 4-6 last ten while scoring 8.6 runs a game.

Milwaukee comes in 6-4 over its last ten at 5.6 runs a game, riding a W2 streak, and its 40-28 road record is better than Chicago's 40-31 mark at home. The Cubs are on an L2 and haven't looked like a team that can stop anyone lately. None of that cancels the central point, though. If Henderson leaves a few pitches up in the zone at Wrigley, the ballpark and the lineup will do the rest, and recent form won't matter.

If It Gets to the Bullpens

The power has to show up early, because Milwaukee's relief corps is the better one

The secondary story is what happens once Henderson hands the ball off. Milwaukee's bullpen carries a 3.15 ERA across 494.0 relief innings against Chicago's 3.93 across 307.0, and that is a meaningful gap in a game that will probably be decided by whoever holds a lead in the seventh. The Cubs' relievers do miss more bats, 9.53 strikeouts per nine to the Brewers' 9.04, but run prevention favors the visitors. In a game the Cubs will likely need to win with a crooked number, the innings after the starter are where Milwaukee's structural edge shows up.

That's why the power angle has a clock on it. Chicago's best chance to launch is against the starter, and Henderson's flyball tendency is a real crack in a 2.48 ERA. Get to him early, get a couple of balls into the bleachers, and the Cubs can put Milwaukee's bullpen in a hole rather than in a save situation. Fail to do that, and 197 home runs on the season won't help much against a relief corps with a 3.15 ERA.

The clock

The homers have to come off Henderson, because a 3.15 bullpen ERA is waiting behind him.

Why

What would change it

  • Henderson gets pulled early on pitch count and the game becomes a bullpen contest before the Brewers' relief edge matters.
  • The Cubs' lineup keeps the 8.6 runs a game pace from its last ten and simply outslugs a Brewers offense that ranks 29th in homers.
