Sale's Strikeouts Are the Story

The Dodgers can't do damage on pitches they never touch

Chris Sale struck out 11.07 batters per nine innings in the regular season, and that's the whole premise of Game 3. With this Division Series tied 1 to 1 in a best of 5, the Dodgers walk into Truist Park needing to put the ball in play against a pitcher who spent the summer refusing to let anyone do it. He piled up 200 strikeouts across 162.2 innings and 27 starts. A strikeout is the one outcome a good lineup can't salvage: no seeing eye single, no productive out, no error to lean on.

The run prevention followed the whiffs. Sale finished the regular season with a 2.16 ERA, and over his last 4 starts it was 1.95 across 27.2 innings, so there's no sign of the late season fade that sometimes catches a veteran arm. That matters because the Dodgers aren't a lineup you can pitch to contact against. When the ball goes in play, they hurt you; the job tonight is keeping it out of play, and neither starter does that better than Sale.

The edge

Every strikeout is a plate appearance the Dodgers' power never gets to use.

No Walks, No Easy Traffic

The whiffs don't come with a walk tax

Strikeout pitchers usually pay for the whiffs with walks. Sale doesn't: he walked 1.72 per nine in the regular season, which leaves a hitter almost nothing to work with once he falls behind. His WHIP was 0.98, and opponents managed only a .215 batting average against him. A lineup that wants to grind a pitch count has to earn every baserunner the hard way.

The platoon numbers close the last door. Sale allowed a .595 OPS to left-handed hitters and a .570 OPS to right-handed hitters, so stacking one side of the lineup doesn't buy the Dodgers much. Yoshinobu Yamamoto is excellent too, with a 2.53 ERA and 2.04 walks per nine, but he struck out 8.85 per nine; he wins with weak contact, not with empty swings. That difference is this matchup in miniature.

The edge

Sale won't walk you and won't let you square him up, which leaves the Dodgers one plan: swing and connect.

The Lineup That Has to Make Contact

Elite power meets the worst possible opponent for it

None of this means the Dodgers are easy to silence. They posted a .762 team OPS in the regular season, 2nd in the majors, scored 801 runs and hit 204 home runs. That production drove a +201 run differential, comfortably the better margin in this game; Atlanta finished at +116.

The catch is that the Dodgers were a touch worse against left-handed pitching, a .756 OPS against lefties next to a .766 OPS against right-handers. It's a small gap, but it runs in Sale's direction. Power needs contact to exist, and a lineup built on 204 home runs gets fewer chances to use it when the pitcher on the mound strikes out 11.07 per nine.

The edge

The Dodgers' power is real; it just needs the bat to find the ball first.

Atlanta Has Won the Low Scoring Games

The meetings have been tight, and tight favors the strikeout arm

The Braves took 6 of the 8 meetings this season, and the Dodgers went 2-6 in them, with the runs reading 22-32 and the Dodgers on the short end. The August games at Truist Park were all decided by a single run: 3-4, 5-6 and a 0-1 night when the Dodgers didn't score at all. This lineup has not found the building easy.

That's the environment Sale thrives in: low totals, one swing deciding the night, and a pitcher who removes balls in play holding the most leverage. The Braves also went 52-29 at home in the regular season, so the setting leans their way. The Division Series itself sits 1 to 1, which means the template from the summer is exactly what tonight comes back to.

The edge

When Atlanta has beaten this lineup, it has done it in low scoring games, and low scoring games belong to strikeout pitchers.

What Could Break the Script

Sale has to carry the run prevention himself

The Dodgers arrive in better shape on paper. They went 7-3 over their last 10 completed games and scored 4.1 runs per game in that stretch, after a 49-32 road record in the regular season; the Braves went 5-5 and scored 4 per game. Form like that doesn't evaporate because of one starter.

The way Sale loses this game is the way strikeout pitchers usually lose: counts that run long, an early exit, and an Atlanta offense with only a .718 team OPS in the regular season left to cover the gap. The Braves ranked 17th in OPS, so they won't bail him out with a crooked number. That's why the strikeouts matter so much here; Sale has to be the run suppression, and his 11.07 per nine says he can be.

The edge

Atlanta's bats won't carry this game. Sale's strikeouts have to.

  • Yamamoto matches Sale inning for inning and the weaker Atlanta offense can't push a run across.
