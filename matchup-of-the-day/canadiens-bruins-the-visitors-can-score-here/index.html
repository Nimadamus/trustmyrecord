Montreal Arrives With an Attack That Travels

A respectable offence walks into one of the stingiest buildings in hockey.

The Canadiens come into TD Garden scoring 3.44 goals a game, 13th of 32 in the league, and they meet a Boston defence allowing just 2.28, 2nd of 32. That's the whole question here: does a middle of the pack attack bend against one of the stingiest teams in hockey, or does it find its usual quota? Montreal sits at 11-3-4 and Boston at 13-4-1, so this isn't a mismatch of records. It's a mismatch of profiles, and the profile that matters most is how the visitors' scoring holds up in this building.

The case that Montreal can score here starts with where it has done its best work. The Canadiens are 6-1-1 on the road against 5-2-3 at home, so the attack is no home ice illusion. Boston's 2.28 goals allowed a game is a real wall, and nobody should expect a track meet. The question is whether Montreal can get near its own 3.44, not whether it blows past it.

The edge

Montreal scores 3.44 goals a game; Boston allows 2.28. Something has to give.

Why Boston's Goals Against Number Is So Low

The league's best team save percentage does much of the work.

Boston's team save percentage of .921 ranks 1st of 32, and it does a lot of the work behind the 2.28 goals allowed a game. That's the number that most directly threatens Montreal's attack, because pucks that go in elsewhere tend to stay out here. Jeremy Swayman is listed to start for Boston, and that save percentage belongs to the whole team, not to any one goalie. At home the Bruins are 8-1-0, which tells you how rarely visitors have solved this building so far.

Then there's the other crease. Montreal's team save percentage is .896, 12th of 32, a clear tier below Boston's .921 team save percentage. Both clubs have 2 shutouts, each ranked 2nd of 32, so neither defence is incapable of a clean sheet. The gap in goal is why the margins look so different even though the attacks are closer than you'd guess.

The wall

A team save percentage of .921, 1st of 32, is what Montreal has to climb.

Montreal Finishes the Chances It Gets

Efficient shooting is the engine behind the visitors' scoring.

Montreal shoots 12 percent, 10th of 32, and that's the engine behind 3.44 goals a game. It's efficient finishing without being an outlier, and efficient finishing travels better than a streak built on luck. Against Boston's team save percentage of .921 that conversion rate will be tested. Still, it's the clearest reason to believe Montreal's scoring is real rather than borrowed.

Boston is sharper still, shooting 13.9 percent, 3rd of 32, and scoring 3.78 goals a game, 6th of 32. That matters to the central story in a roundabout way. Montreal allows 3.06 a game, 15th of 32, so the Canadiens probably have to score at their own rate just to stay level. Their attack isn't only an opportunity here; it's a requirement.

The edge

Shooting 12 percent, Montreal doesn't need luck to score.

The Penalty Kill Is the Crack in Boston's Defence

A weak power play meets a middling kill.

The one place Montreal's attack hasn't shown up is the power play, which converts at 14.8 percent, 30th of 32. That would usually be a worry against a top defence. But Boston's penalty kill sits at 77 percent, 17th of 32, the softest piece of the Bruins' defensive profile. If the Canadiens are going to beat that 2.28 goals allowed average, a man advantage against a mid table kill is the most obvious opening.

Boston's own power play is no weapon either, 18.3 percent and 24th of 32, while Montreal kills at 77.8 percent, 15th of 32. So special teams look closer to a wash than the overall goal numbers suggest. That pushes the game back toward even strength, where Boston's goaltending and finishing have carried it all season.

The opening

Boston's 77 percent penalty kill, 17th of 32, is where Montreal's attack can get in.

Montreal's Form Says the Goals Will Come

Points in every recent game, but a much thinner margin.

Montreal comes in 7-0-3 over its last ten, without a regulation loss in that stretch, and Boston is 7-2-1. The standings barely separate them, 27 points for Boston and 26 for Montreal. The Canadiens' form says they keep finding ways to collect points, and that's the strongest argument that their attack shows up here too.

Where the clubs split apart is the margin. Boston's goal differential is +1.5 a game, 2nd of 32, and +27 overall, while Montreal sits at +0.38 a game, 11th of 32, and +7 overall. Montreal can score at TD Garden. The harder question is whether it can contain Boston's 3.78 goals a game at the other end.
