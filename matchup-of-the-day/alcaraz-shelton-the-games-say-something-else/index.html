The Games Say Something Else

Down at the game level, these two are not close.

Look at the two records and this reads like a fair fight. Alcaraz is 26-4 on the season, Shelton is 37-13, and both of them have played their way into the quarterfinals in New York without dropping a match. But a match record is a coarse instrument; it counts a scrappy escape exactly the same as a rout. Go down to the game level and the two of them come apart.

Alcaraz has won 60.6% of the games he has played this year, 456 of them against 296 lost, for a differential of +160. Shelton sits at 53.6%, 796 won and 688 lost, a differential of +108 across a great deal more tennis. That gap is not a rounding artifact over that many games. It is the difference between a man who is usually ahead inside the set and a man who is usually level in it, and neither the 86.7% match win rate nor Shelton's 74% quite tells you that.

The edge

Alcaraz wins 60.6% of the games he plays, Shelton 53.6%, and 26-4 against 37-13 hides every bit of it.

What 26-4 and 37-13 Hide

Matches, sets, games: the same verdict, louder each time.

The sets say the same thing one step up from the games. Alcaraz has won 64 sets and lost 13, a set win rate of 83.1%. Shelton has won 93 and lost 48, which is 66%. Shelton's raw totals are bigger because he has played 18 tournaments to Alcaraz's 7, and playing that much and winning 37 times is its own achievement, but volume does not move a rate.

Stack the layers and they all lean the same way with different force. Matches, 86.7% against 74%. Sets, 83.1% against 66%. Games, 60.6% against 53.6%. The deeper you go, the closer you get to what is actually happening out there on a point by point basis, and the deeper you go, the more of Shelton's season looks like hard labour rather than control.

The edge

83.1% of sets to 66%, and 60.6% of games to 53.6%. Every layer under the record widens the gap instead of closing it.

Both Own the Hard Courts, One of Them More Than the Other

The only surface record that matters tonight.

This is a hard court match at a Grand Slam, so the hard court numbers are the ones with a vote. Alcaraz is 21-2 on hard this season, a win rate of 91.3%. Shelton is 23-6 on the same surface, 79.3%. Both of those are seriously good; only one of them is close to unbeatable, and it is the one whose game win share is already the higher of the two.

Neither man has been dragged around at this event. Alcaraz is 4-0 at this US Open, Shelton is 4-0 as well, and the draw has done neither of them a favour in the only way that counts, which is that they now have to play each other rather than somebody else. The rankings agree with the rest of it: Alcaraz at No. 3 on 7160 points, Shelton at No. 9 on 3480.

The edge

21-2 on hard this season against 23-6. The surface Shelton is best on is the surface Alcaraz has barely lost on.

How Each of Them Actually Finishes a Match

Shelton's route in, and why it runs through breakers.

The way each of them wins falls straight out of the game numbers. Alcaraz has closed 20 of his 26 wins in straight sets, 76.9% of them. Shelton has 18 straight sets wins from 37, which is 48.6%. Win 60.6% of your games and you rarely need a deciding set; win 53.6% and you live in them.

Shelton is not fragile in the deep water, though, and this is where his case genuinely sits. He is 15-9 in matches that went to a deciding set and 25-13 in tiebreaks, a 65.8% tiebreak rate that is the single best number either man brings to this. Alcaraz is 4-1 in deciders off a much smaller sample and only 7-6 in tiebreaks, 53.8%. Best of 5 sets hands Shelton the format he wants, more sets and more chances that one of them ends up in a breaker where the season long game gap stops mattering for about ten minutes.

The edge

Shelton is 25-13 in tiebreaks at 65.8%. He needs this decided in them, because he will not win it in the games.

Form, Titles and the Shape of Two Very Different Years

Shelton is hot. Alcaraz has been picking his weeks.

By the crude measure Shelton is the hotter player, 9-1 across his last ten matches to Alcaraz's 8-2. He has 4 titles this season to Alcaraz's 2, and he is 4-0 in finals while Alcaraz is 2-1. Nobody should wave that away; a man who does not lose finals has a spine, and 37-13 is the best year of his life.

It also came out of 18 tournaments against 7. Shelton has been on the road all season stacking weeks, Alcaraz has shown up almost exclusively for the big ones and gone 26-4 doing it. A quarterfinal in New York is the week that tends to sort those two approaches out, and both of them walk into it 4-0 here.

The edge

4 titles from 18 events against 2 titles from 7. Two good seasons, built on completely different amounts of tennis.

Carlos Alcaraz

Confidence · Strong

The game level gap is the widest thing on this page and it points one way, so Alcaraz is the side, with the caveat that Shelton's breaker record makes a long night plausible.

Why

  • Alcaraz wins 60.6% of the games he plays to Shelton's 53.6%, and that separation is larger and more stable than the two match records imply.
  • On hard courts specifically, Alcaraz is 21-2 for 91.3% against 23-6 and 79.3%, so the surface does nothing to close the gap.
  • Alcaraz has finished 76.9% of his wins in straight sets against 48.6% for Shelton, which is what a game advantage that size usually looks like on a scoreboard.

What would change it

  • If the match reaches a deciding set, Shelton's 15-9 record there and his 25-13 tiebreak mark start to matter far more than the season long game share.
  • Alcaraz is only 7-6 in tiebreaks, so a match that turns into a run of breakers takes his biggest edge off the table.
  • Shelton is 9-1 across his last ten and 4-0 in finals this season, and form that hot has beaten better season numbers before.
