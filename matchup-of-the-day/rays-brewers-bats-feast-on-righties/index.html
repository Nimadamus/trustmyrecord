The Rays Hit Right-Handers Like a Different Team

Tampa Bay's platoon gap, and why it matters in this game

The Rays come into Game 2 of a best of 7 World Series with the Milwaukee Brewers leading the series 1 to 0, and the most useful thing to know about their lineup is which arm it's facing. In the regular season Tampa Bay posted a .752 OPS against right-handed pitching and a .679 OPS against left-handers. That isn't a quirk of a thin sample: the right-handed figure comes from 4134 plate appearances and the left-handed one from 1972. Logan Henderson throws right-handed, so the Rays get the matchup they've handled best all year.

Put the split next to the full season and it gets sharper. The Rays' overall regular season OPS was .728, 8th in baseball, which means the righty number is carrying the whole line while the lefty number drags it down. At a .752 OPS against right-handers this lineup plays like a top offense; at a .679 OPS against lefties it's ordinary. The question for Game 2 isn't whether this club can hit, it's whether Henderson is the kind of right-hander the split actually applies to.

The Right-Hander Standing in the Way

Logan Henderson was hardest on exactly the hitters the split favors

Here's the catch with leaning on a platoon split: Henderson was brutal on right-handed hitters in the regular season, allowing a .545 OPS to them and a .592 OPS to left-handers. He went 11-3 with a 2.47 ERA across 18 starts and 98.1 innings, with a 0.84 WHIP and 10.07 strikeouts per nine. Opponents managed a .183 batting average against him over 371 batters faced. So the Rays' best split is running into a pitcher who erased most hitters regardless of which side they stood on.

He also walked only 1.74 per nine, so Tampa Bay won't be building innings on free passes. That puts the whole burden on contact, and contact against right-handers is exactly where the Rays' .752 OPS lives. The other wrinkle is volume: 98.1 innings is a light regular season workload, which hints the Rays could see Milwaukee's bullpen sooner than they'd see it behind a workhorse. His last 3 starts produced a 2.4 ERA, but they were spread out over weeks, so treat that as a small sample and not as form.

Henderson held right-handed hitters to a .545 OPS, and that's the wall the Rays' best split has to climb.

Milwaukee Has a Righty Split of Its Own

The bigger numbers favor the Brewers; the matchup narrows them

It cuts both ways. Milwaukee posted a .759 OPS against right-handed pitching in the regular season, a touch above Tampa Bay's .752 OPS, and a .706 OPS against lefties. The Brewers' overall .744 OPS ranked 3rd and their 832 runs ranked 2nd, which is how a club with only 154 home runs, 28th in the league, still finished with a run differential of +214. They don't need the long ball to score, and that profile travels well into a tight October game.

Tampa Bay's run differential was +86 on 736 runs scored and 650 allowed, the line of a good team rather than a dominant one. The Rays' case was never going to come from the season totals; it comes from the specific matchup. Against a right-hander, Tampa Bay's lineup played almost exactly like Milwaukee's did, .752 OPS to .759 OPS. That's why the split matters more than the tape: it shrinks a big gap on paper down to one night of contact.

What the Meetings Have Shown So Far

Tampa Bay keeps getting stuck on the same run total

The meetings haven't been kind to the Rays. Tampa Bay is 1-3 against Milwaukee this season, Game 1 of this World Series included, and has been outscored 9-19 across those 4 games. Their only win was the 3-2 opener back in March. In each of the 3 losses since, the Rays scored exactly 2 runs, which is the clearest sign that the split hasn't shown up against this staff yet.

Game 1 was the tightest of those losses, a 2-3 finish, and it said more about how thin the margins are than about any platoon edge. Every one of those meetings was played at American Family Field, where Milwaukee went 55-26 in the regular season against Tampa Bay's 43-38 road record. If the righty split is going to matter, it has to matter in the building where the Brewers have been hardest to beat.

Why the Split Has to Pay Early

Rasmussen keeps it close; the bullpens decide who cashes in

Tampa Bay's half of the argument is Drew Rasmussen keeping the score small long enough for the split to matter. He went 16-5 with a 2.67 ERA over 172.0 innings in the regular season, with a 0.91 WHIP and opponents batting .199 against him. He walked 1.67 per nine, so Milwaukee won't get free runners either. If he does his part, this is a game decided by a handful of swings.

After the starters, the edge leans Milwaukee: a 3.08 bullpen ERA in the regular season against Tampa Bay's 3.51, with the Brewers striking out 9.44 per nine to the Rays' 9.1. That's why the Rays' split has to pay off against Henderson, not later against relievers. If Tampa Bay's .752 OPS against right-handers turns into runs the first time or two through the order, Rasmussen can protect a lead. If it doesn't, the late innings tilt toward the home bullpen.

The clock

The Rays' split has to show up against Henderson, because a 3.08 bullpen ERA is waiting behind him.
