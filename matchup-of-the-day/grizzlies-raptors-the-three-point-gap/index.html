The Gap Beyond the Arc

Memphis shoots 37.8 percent from three this season; Toronto shoots 31.5.

Strip this matchup down to a single line and it reads like this: Memphis makes 37.8 percent of its threes this season and Toronto makes 31.5 percent. That's the 4th best three point percentage in the league against the 27th, out of 30 clubs, and it isn't the kind of gap you explain away with one hot week. Both teams are nine games in, Memphis at 6-3 and Toronto at 5-4, so the sample is young, but it's the same length for each side.

What the figure doesn't tell you is volume, because nothing behind this page counts attempts. So the honest read is about efficiency: whenever the Grizzlies let one fly, the return has been meaningfully better than Toronto's. Over a full game that compounds, and in a matchup where the two offenses are otherwise close, the arc is where the separation lives.

The edge

4th of 30 against 27th of 30 from three isn't noise; it's the shape of this game.

Two Offenses, Two Routes to the Same Total

Toronto scores slightly more this season, but it gets there inside the arc.

Here's the wrinkle. Toronto actually scores a touch more this season, 118.2 points a game to 117.9 for Memphis, ranking 8th and 9th of 30. The Raptors also shoot better from the field overall, 46.2 percent field goal percentage against 45.8, 13th against 16th. If they trail this badly from deep and still lead on overall field goal percentage, they're doing their damage inside the arc.

That fits the rest of the Toronto profile. The Raptors average 28.7 assists a game, 3rd of 30, and turn it over only 14 times a game, which ranks 8th. That's a team building good looks closer to the rim and moving the ball to get there. Memphis lands on almost the same scoring total with a 37.8 percent three point stroke doing a lot of the lifting, and that's a more portable kind of offense away from home.

The wrinkle

Toronto scores 118.2 a game while shooting 31.5 percent from three. The damage is coming from somewhere else.

The Free Throw Line Backs Up the Three Point Line

Memphis ranks 1st in free throw percentage; Toronto ranks 27th in both.

The three point split isn't an isolated number; it rhymes with what happens at the line. Memphis makes 83.9 percent of its free throws this season, 1st of 30, while Toronto sits at 74.2 percent, 27th. The Raptors rank 27th in both three point percentage and free throw percentage, which is about as consistent a shooting signal as a young sample can give you.

Free throw percentage is often treated as the cleanest read on pure touch, because nobody is contesting the shot. When a team ranks 1st there and 4th from three, the long range number looks less like luck and more like identity. For Toronto, the matching 27th rankings suggest the misses from deep aren't just bad variance either, which matters for anyone expecting a home bounce back.

Second witness

Rebounding Feeds the Main Edge

Memphis averages 48.8 rebounds a game this season; Toronto averages 42.2.

Rebounding is the secondary thread, and it supports the main one. Memphis pulls down 48.8 rebounds a game this season, 2nd of 30, against 42.2 for Toronto, which ranks 23rd. This data doesn't split offensive from defensive boards, so treat it as a broad edge on the glass rather than a precise count of second chances.

Still, the logic holds. A team that shoots well from deep and also controls more of the misses gets more chances to let its best weapon work. Toronto's rim protection is thin as well, 3.9 blocks a game, 26th, against 5.1 and 12th for Memphis, so the Raptors aren't erasing much at the other end to compensate.

Support

2nd of 30 on the glass against 23rd: the shooters on the better side also get more of the misses.

Toronto's Way Back In Is the Steal

The Raptors rank 2nd in steals; Memphis ranks 19th in turnovers.

Toronto's best answer isn't shooting; it's taking the ball away. The Raptors average 11.6 steals a game this season, 2nd of 30, and Memphis gives it away 15.8 times a game, 19th. Every turnover is a trip where the Grizzlies never get to launch a three at all, so this is the lever that most directly blunts the central edge.

The attack against defence thread points the same way. Toronto's 118.2 points a game meets a Memphis defence allowing 111.8, 11th of 30, so the Raptors' offense is good enough to keep pace if it isn't bleeding value at the arc. Memphis, for its part, ranks 29th in steals at 6.1 a game, so it won't create much chaos to disrupt Toronto's ball movement.

The counter

11.6 steals a game is how Toronto turns a shooting deficit into a possession surplus.

Where It Tilts

Even margins, an unbeaten road record, and one clear skill gap.

Location usually favors the home side, but this season's sample pushes back. Memphis is 4-0 on the road and 2-3 at home, while Toronto is 2-1 at Scotiabank Arena and 3-3 away. Those are tiny splits and nobody should build a case on them, but they do remove the assumption that travel hurts the Grizzlies.

The scoring margins are close: +6.1 a game for Memphis, 8th of 30, and +5.4 for Toronto, 11th. When two clubs grade this evenly, the tiebreaker is the clearest skill gap between them, and that's 37.8 percent from three against 31.5. Toronto can steal its way into this game, but if both sides shoot to form, Memphis has the more reliable path.

The tiebreaker

With margins of +6.1 and +5.4, the arc decides it: 37.8 percent against 31.5.
