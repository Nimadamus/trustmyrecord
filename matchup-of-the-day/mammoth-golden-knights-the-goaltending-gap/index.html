The gap is in the crease

Two good offences, one working goal line

Start with the one number that separates these clubs more than any other. Vegas has a .902 team save percentage through 19 games, 6th of 32. Utah's team save percentage is .868 through 18 games, 31st of 32. Nearly the whole league sits between them in goal, and that's the game.

What makes the gap matter is how little else separates them. Utah is 10-7-1 and scores 3.5 goals a game, which is a good team's offence. It also allows 3.5 a game, so the goal differential is exactly 0. A club that scores like that and only breaks even is telling you where the leak is.

The edge

Vegas ranks 6th of 32 in team save percentage at .902, and Utah ranks 31st of 32 in team save percentage at .868.

What an .868 save percentage costs Utah

A strong attack that only breaks even

The Mammoth rank 8th of 32 in goals per game and 25th of 32 in goals allowed per game. Put those together and you get a differential that ranks 17th of 32, dead in the middle. That's the signature of an .868 team save percentage: a strong attack paying for what happens at the other end. The last ten, 4-5-1, reads the same way.

There's one honest wrinkle. Utah has 1 shutout, which ranks 7th of 32, so the floor isn't always the result. But a single clean night across 18 games doesn't offset a team save percentage that ranks 31st of 32. The typical outing is the one the 3.5 goals allowed per game describes.

The cost

Utah is 8th of 32 in goals per game and 17th of 32 in goal differential per game. The distance between those ranks is the goaltending.

Vegas wins with steadiness, not shutouts

A .902 team save percentage with no blankings behind it

Vegas allows 2.68 goals a game, 5th of 32, and the .902 team save percentage is the plainest explanation for it in this dataset. The Golden Knights haven't done it with spectacular nights either: they have 0 shutouts, 18th of 32. This is steadiness, not a few blankings propping up an average. It adds up to a +13 goal differential and +0.69 a game, 6th of 32.

Carter Hart is listed to start for Vegas and Karel Vejmelka is listed to start for Utah. Treat both as listings, and note that the save percentages here belong to each team as a whole, not to either goalie. Still, the team numbers are what we've got, and they all point the same direction.

The profile

Vegas allows 2.68 goals a game, 5th of 32, without a single shutout. The average is built on ordinary nights.

The offences cancel, so the stopping decides it

Similar scoring, similar finishing, different resistance

Both clubs score: 3.5 goals a game for Utah and 3.37 for Vegas, 8th and 13th of 32. They finish at almost identical rates too, with Utah's shooting percentage at 11.7, 13th of 32, and Vegas's at 11.6, 14th of 32. So the attacks are close to a wash. When the scoring ends are that similar, the stopping ends decide it.

The interesting collision is Utah's 3.5 goals a game against a defence allowing 2.68. Something gives there, and a .902 team save percentage is a better bet to hold its shape than an .868 team save percentage is to suddenly find one. Vegas's finishing, meanwhile, gets the softer assignment. Same shooting, very different resistance.

The matchup

Shooting percentages of 11.7 and 11.6 say the finishing is even. The goals allowed, 3.5 against 2.68, say the rest isn't.

Utah's way back in runs through the power play

The one matchup that tilts toward the Mammoth

If Utah wins this, the power play is probably why. It converts at 37.1 percent, 2nd of 32, and Vegas kills penalties at 76.5 percent, 19th of 32. That's the one matchup on the board that tilts clearly toward the Mammoth. A man advantage is how a goaltending deficit gets outrun.

The trouble is that the same logic runs the other way, and it runs harder. Utah's penalty kill is at 69.6 percent, 27th of 32, and Vegas's power play is at 26.3 percent, 9th of 32. A penalty kill leans on its goaltending, and Utah's sits in front of an .868 team save percentage. A special teams game helps the Mammoth only if they're the ones drawing the calls.

The counter

Utah's power play, 37.1 percent and 2nd of 32, is real. So is a penalty kill at 69.6 percent, 27th of 32.

Nothing in the records argues with the goaltending

Records, venue splits and recent form

Vegas is 12-5-2 with 26 standings points and 6-3-0 at home. Utah is 10-7-1 with 21 standings points and 6-5-1 on the road, having been better in its own building at 4-2-0. Recent form is 5-3-2 over the last ten for the Golden Knights and 4-5-1 for the Mammoth. None of it contradicts the numbers in goal.

The caution is sample size. These are 18 and 19 game profiles, and early profiles can move. But the question for this game isn't whether Utah's .868 team save percentage is permanent. It's whether any figure in front of us suggests it changes on the road against a club scoring 3.37 goals a game, and there isn't one.

The caveat

An .868 team save percentage over 18 games isn't a verdict on a season. It's still the number Utah carries into this one.
