The Free Base Is Off the Table

Walks are where most offenses get their cheap runs, and Martinez doesn't hand them out.

Start with the number that defines the matchup. In the regular season, Nick Martinez walked 1.30 batters per nine innings across 180.0 innings. That's a pitcher who treats the strike zone as home and dares you to do something with it.

His WHIP of 1.09 follows from that. Few walks means few traffic jams, and the innings that blow up usually start with a free pass. Cleveland can't count on one.

The edge

Martinez gives you nothing for free. Every Guardians baserunner has to be earned.

He Pitches to Contact, and That's the Point

Low strikeouts and low walks add up to a pitcher who lives on weak contact.

Martinez isn't a strikeout arm. He struck out 5.45 per nine, which is modest, so he's asking his defense to do real work. It worked: a 3.10 ERA and a 15-6 record in the regular season, with opponents hitting .249 against him.

The splits don't give a lineup a clean way in either. He allowed a .659 OPS to left-handed hitters and a .705 OPS to right-handed hitters. Neither side of the plate has a soft spot to attack, so Cleveland can't simply stack the lineup one way and expect it to matter.

The profile

No strikeout spike, no walk problem, no platoon hole. It's a pitcher with very few ways to lose.

Armstrong Is the Opposite Kind of Arm

Cleveland's starter walks far more batters, and that gap is the whole story.

Shawn Armstrong takes the ball for Cleveland, and his regular season line looks nothing like Martinez's. He walked 4.84 per nine across 35.1 innings, against Martinez's 1.30. He has not started a game this season, so treat that line as a small sample, not a settled one.

He does miss bats, with 8.66 strikeouts per nine and a 4.58 ERA. He allowed a .657 OPS to left-handed hitters and a .748 OPS to right-handed hitters. The strikeouts are real, but the walks hand Tampa Bay baserunners without a swing, which is the exact thing Martinez refuses to do.

The gap

One starter takes the free base away. The other keeps offering it.

The Rest of the Ledger Is Close

Neither lineup nor staff separates these clubs the way the starters might.

Over the regular season, Tampa Bay posted a .728 team OPS to Cleveland's .696. The Rays' staff ERA was 3.70 and Cleveland's was 3.77, so the pitching staffs are nearly even.

Where the clubs split is run differential. The Rays outscored opponents by +86 in the regular season, while Cleveland finished at +11. That gap matters here because a pitcher who limits free baserunners is the sort of starter who protects a margin, and Martinez is the one who does it.

The context

The staffs are level. The starters are not.

Where Game 4 Sits in the Series

Cleveland leads, and Tampa Bay needs a clean start from its pitcher.

This is Game 4 of the League Championship Series, a best of 7, and the Guardians lead the series 2 to 1. The Rays took the regular season meetings 6-3, but those games don't carry into October. Cleveland won the last meeting 3-2 at home, which keeps the margin thin.

In a game this tight, command is what keeps a start from getting away. If Martinez keeps the walks off the board, Cleveland has to string together hits against a pitcher who works inside the zone. If Armstrong falls behind hitters, Tampa Bay gets what Martinez never gives.

The swing

Walks decide a game like this. Only one side is likely to issue them.
