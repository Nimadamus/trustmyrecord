The Gap That Defines This Matchup

One offence finishes drives, the other too often doesn't.

Start with the number this whole matchup turns on. Baltimore has turned 73.7 percent of its red zone trips into touchdowns, which ranks 5th in the league. Cleveland's offense sits at 45.5 percent, which ranks 31st. Both are five game samples, so neither figure is settled, but the gap is wide enough that closing it would take a sharp correction on both sides at once.

This split matters because it measures what happens after the hard part is done. Reaching the red zone takes a whole drive's work; Cleveland gets there and too often leaves with a field goal or nothing. Baltimore treats a red zone possession as something close to a touchdown in hand. Over four quarters that compounds, because a team that finishes drives simply doesn't need as many of them.

The edge

Baltimore scores touchdowns on 73.7 percent of red zone trips; Cleveland's offense does it 45.5 percent of the time.

The Scoreboard Follows the Finishing

Points per game look like the red zone split carried forward.

The scoring lines read like a direct consequence of the red zone numbers. The Ravens average 29.2 points a game; the Browns average 18.6. Baltimore is 4-1 with a +27 point differential, while Cleveland is 3-2 despite a -19 differential. That tells you the Browns have been winning games without reliably scoring their way into them.

Yardage explains part of the gap, though not all of it. Baltimore gains 410.6 yards a game, 5th in the league, and Cleveland gains 296.2, which ranks 29th. A yardage gap that size would open a scoring gap by itself. Finishing is what widens it: the Ravens don't just get more chances, they also convert a far bigger share of them, 73.7 percent against 45.5.

The edge

Cleveland is 3-2 with a -19 point differential; the record has run ahead of the scoring.

Why Baltimore Can Lean Forward Near the Goal Line

The rushing attack that moves the ball also finishes drives.

Baltimore finishes drives for the same reason it moves the ball at all. The Ravens run for 164.4 yards a game, 3rd in the league, at 5.1 yards a carry, which also ranks 3rd. Inside the red zone the field compresses and throwing windows shrink. The offences that can still push forward on the ground are the ones that score touchdowns instead of kicking.

Cleveland doesn't have that lever. The Browns run for 94.2 yards a game, 21st, at 3.7 yards a carry, which ranks 23rd. When a short field calls for a yard or two at a time, an offence averaging 3.7 a carry is asking a lot of every snap. That fits a red zone rate that sits well behind Baltimore's 73.7 percent.

The edge

Baltimore runs for 5.1 yards a carry; Cleveland manages 3.7.

Third Down Decides How Often Cleveland Even Gets a Look

Two filters stack before the Browns ever reach scoring range.

Third down is where a drive either reaches the red zone or dies short of it, and the Browns struggle here too. Cleveland converts 30.6 percent of third downs, which ranks 31st. Baltimore sits at 38.5 percent, 15th, which is ordinary rather than elite. That middling rate is worth noting honestly: the Ravens' red zone success isn't just a product of sustaining endless drives.

The first down numbers complete the picture. The Ravens average 21.6 first downs a game against 16.2 for the Browns. Fewer first downs means fewer trips into scoring range, and Cleveland then converts the trips it does earn at a lower rate. That's two filters stacked on top of each other, and both point the same way.

The edge

Cleveland converts 30.6 percent of third downs, 31st in the league, so it reaches the red zone less often before it ever has to finish.

The Pass Rush Is Cleveland's Best Answer

Sacks are the quickest way to turn a touchdown trip into a kick.

If Cleveland wins this, the pass rush is the likeliest reason. The Browns have 16 sacks, 7th in the league, and Baltimore throws for 229.8 yards a game, 14th. A sack on a red zone possession is the fastest way to turn a touchdown trip into a field goal try. That's exactly the kind of play that could drag the Ravens' finishing rate back toward the middle.

The problem is that Baltimore's passing is efficient when it does throw. The Ravens complete 73 percent of their passes, 1st in the league, at 7.76 yards an attempt. They've given the ball away just 3 times for a +3 turnover differential, against Cleveland's -1, and the Browns have only 6 takeaways. Cleveland is 2-0 at home and Baltimore is 3-0 on the road, but neither split is big enough to outweigh the efficiency gap.

The edge

The Browns have 16 sacks, and that's the one lever that can turn Baltimore touchdowns into field goals.
