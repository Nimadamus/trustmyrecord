The Down That Decides Everything

Best in the league against 29th

San Francisco converts 51.9 percent of its third downs, and that ranks 1st in the league. Washington converts 31.9 percent, which ranks 29th. Those two numbers are the whole game: one offense stays on the field and the other keeps sending its punter out. It's still only five games for each team, so read these as early season rates and not settled truth.

The first down counts point the same way. The 49ers average 20.6 first downs a game and Washington averages 18.6. That gap looks small until you ask where the conversions come from. A first down on second and short is routine, but one on third and seven keeps alive a drive that would usually die. San Francisco keeps winning that exact down, and that's how a team gets to 5-0.

The edge

San Francisco converts 51.9 percent on third down, Washington 31.9, and nearly every other number in this game follows from that.

Why Washington Stalls Behind the Sticks

A strong ground game with nothing behind it

Washington's third down trouble starts in the passing game. The Commanders complete 58.6 percent of their throws, 30th in the league, and average 5.46 yards per attempt, 20th. Third and medium asks a quarterback to move the ball through the air when everyone knows the pass is coming. An offense throwing for 187.8 yards a game, 24th, doesn't have the efficiency to do that reliably, and the 31.9 percent conversion rate is what you get.

The frustrating part for Washington is that early downs aren't the problem. They run for 139.6 yards a game, 6th, at 4.7 a carry, 7th, which is genuinely top of the league work. That running game should be setting up third and short, and sometimes it does. But a run first offense with a 58.6 percent completion rate gets exposed as soon as it falls behind, and against a team scoring 30.8 points a game it will fall behind.

The edge

Running for 139.6 a game wins first down. Completing 58.6 percent of passes loses third down.

How San Francisco Keeps Third Down Short

Accurate passing and a balanced attack

San Francisco's conversion rate is built on an efficient passing game. The 49ers complete 69 percent of their throws, 4th in the league, at 8.15 yards per attempt. That pairing makes third down a math problem the defense keeps losing. Completions move the chains, and the yards per throw mean the third downs they do face are shorter to begin with.

They add 128.6 rushing yards a game, 8th, at 4.6 a carry, 10th. That balance keeps third down manageable, because a defense can't sit on the pass while the run is working. Their 367.8 total yards a game ranks 10th, which isn't eye popping by itself. What matters is that San Francisco's yards come on the downs that keep drives alive, and that's why the 51.9 percent rate looks real.

The edge

Completing 69 percent of passes at 8.15 yards an attempt means San Francisco seldom faces a long third down.

Long Drives That End in Touchdowns

The secondary thread that backs up the main one

Long drives only pay if they end in touchdowns, and this thread backs up the main one. San Francisco turns 87.5 percent of red zone trips into touchdowns, 1st in the league. Washington does it 50 percent of the time, 25th. Convert on third down, finish in the red zone, and you get an offense scoring 30.8 points a game.

Washington's 23 points a game leave a lot less room for error. An offense converting 31.9 percent of its third downs reaches the red zone less often, and when it gets there it scores touchdowns at a 50 percent rate. That's two leaks on the same drive. Meanwhile, the 49ers' 51.9 percent rate means they should have the ball more often to punish both.

The edge

Converting 51.9 percent on third down gets San Francisco there, and an 87.5 percent red zone touchdown rate finishes the job.

The Margin and Washington's One Counterpunch

Point differential against ball security

The 49ers have outscored their opponents by +61 while allowing 18.6 points a game. Washington sits at -34 and gives up 29.8 a game. That spread isn't schedule luck. It's what happens when one team owns the money down and the other doesn't, and San Francisco is 3-0 at home while Washington is 0-2 on the road.

Washington's best argument is ball security. The Commanders carry a +5 turnover differential, 2nd in the league, with just 2 giveaways, ranked 31st on a list where fewer is better. That's a real strength, and it's part of why a team at 1-3-1 hasn't been worse. But holding onto the ball doesn't convert third and six, and a turnover edge built over five games can flip in a single afternoon.

The edge

A +5 turnover differential is Washington's one way back into this game, and it doesn't fix third down.
