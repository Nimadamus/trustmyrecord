The Gap Beyond the Arc

Golden State is among the league's best from three, and Miami is in the middle of the pack.

Golden State is shooting 38.9 percent from three this season and Miami is shooting 34.8 percent. That's a three point percentage ranked 4th of 30 against one ranked 14th of 30, so we're comparing one of the league's better shooting clubs with a thoroughly average one. Both teams have played only eight games, and nobody should treat either figure as settled. Still, it's the first thing you notice when you put these two side by side.

The standings point the same way, with the Warriors at 5-3 and the Heat at 3-5. Every make from deep carries an extra point, so an edge in this column is worth more than the same edge anywhere else on the floor. What we can't tell you is volume: the table reports percentages, not attempts, so it can't say how much of either offense lives out there. That limit matters, because the gap is only as valuable as the number of shots behind it.

The edge

A three point percentage of 38.9 against 34.8 is the cleanest shooting argument Golden State has.

Miami's Answer Is Everything Inside It

The club behind from three is ahead from the field, and ahead on the scoreboard.

Here's the wrinkle. Miami is shooting 47.8 percent from the field, 5th of 30, while Golden State has a 45.5 field goal percentage, 19th of 30. The club that trails from three is well ahead overall. That's why the scoring columns look nothing like the three point columns: the Heat put up 118 points per game, 9th of 30, and the Warriors 117.5, 11th of 30.

So a 38.9 three point percentage hasn't bought Golden State a scoring lead over a club at 34.8. Miami also moves the ball well, with 28.7 assists per game, 4th of 30, against 26.8 for the Warriors, 13th of 30. Read plainly, Golden State's shooting from three is what keeps its offense level with a club that's better from the field. If that percentage slips toward the middle of the table, the field goal column offers no cushion.

The catch

The Warriors win the three point column and still trail Miami, 118 points per game to 117.5.

The Free Throw Line Backs Up the Shooters

The other pure accuracy column leans even harder toward Golden State.

The three point gap has a companion. Golden State is shooting 81.8 percent at the free throw line, 4th of 30, and Miami is shooting 73 percent, 28th of 30. Put that beside the three point ranks, 4th of 30 and 14th of 30, and the Warriors own both columns where a shooter's touch shows up most directly.

This is a statement about accuracy rather than volume, since free throw attempts aren't in the table either. It still matters here, because two offenses at 118 and 117.5 points per game aren't separated by much. A club that makes its threes and its free throws can turn a small lead into a safe one. Miami's free throw percentage gives back points that a close game can't spare.

The companion

A free throw percentage of 81.8 against 73 says the three point gap isn't an isolated quirk.

What the Gap Doesn't Explain

The scoring margins are far apart, and most of that lives on the other end.

The Warriors have a scoring margin of +2.9 per game, 12th of 30, and the Heat are at -4.9, 21st of 30. Over the season so far that's +23 for Golden State and -39 for Miami. With the offenses level at 118 and 117.5 points per game, the three point gap can't be what's producing that spread on its own. The spread comes from points allowed, where Miami gives up 122.9 per game, 27th of 30, and Golden State gives up 114.6, 17th of 30.

That's where the shooting story gets its leverage. A defense allowing 122.9 points per game is a poor place to bring a cold night, and a good one to bring a 38.9 three point percentage. We'd be careful not to stretch it, because the table doesn't split those points by where they came from, and it doesn't show what Miami allows from three. The fair reading is simpler: Golden State's best shooting column meets a club that has struggled to stop anybody.

The margin

Golden State is +2.9 per game and Miami is -4.9, and the difference is mostly defensive.

Chase Center and the Ways It Could Go Wrong

A home edge exists, and turnovers are the threat to it.

The building helps, though the evidence is thin. The Warriors are 3-1 at home and 2-2 on the road, while the Heat are 2-2 on the road and 1-3 at home. Miami's road record is actually better than its home record, so nobody should assume travel sinks them. These are tiny samples, and they don't support much more than a mild lean.

Golden State's real exposure is the ball. It turns it over 15.9 times per game, 20th of 30, against 15.1 for Miami, 17th of 30, and every giveaway is a possession that never reaches the arc. Rebounding gives a little of that back, with the Warriors at 43.9 per game, 16th of 30, and the Heat at 42.3, 22nd of 30. A three point percentage ranked 4th of 30 only pays if the Warriors get the shots up, and at 38.9 they'd take that bet every night.

The risk

At 15.9 turnovers per game, Golden State's biggest threat to its own shooting edge is not getting the shot off.

Why

  • The Warriors hold a clear lead in three point percentage, 38.9 to 34.8.
  • Miami allows 122.9 points per game, 27th of 30, which is a generous setting for a good shooting club.
  • Golden State is also far more accurate at the free throw line and is 3-1 at home.

What would change it

  • Miami closes the three point gap for a night while keeping its field goal percentage near 47.8.
  • Golden State's turnovers run above its 15.9 per game and cost it possessions before it can shoot.
  • The Heat's free throw percentage climbs well above 73 in a close game.
