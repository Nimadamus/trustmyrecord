A Gap in Goal That the Standings Hide

Nearly level in points, nowhere near level in the crease.

Read the standings and you'd call this an even game: Montreal is 9-3-3 with 21 standings points, and Utah is 10-6-0 with 20. Look in the crease and it stops being even. The Canadiens carry a team save percentage of .898, 8th of 32, while the Mammoth carry a team save percentage of .870, 30th of 32. That's a club near the top of the league against a club near the bottom, in the stat that can swing a hockey game faster than almost any other.

Utah has built a 10-6-0 record on top of that .870 save percentage, which tells you how much the rest of its game has been carrying. Montreal's 9-3-3 rests on firmer ground, because its goaltending isn't asking the skaters to bail it out. These are small samples, 15 games for Montreal and 16 for Utah, so neither figure is settled. Still, a gap this size is the best evidence we have about what separates these clubs right now.

The edge

The standings call this even. The crease doesn't.

What Actually Goes In the Net

The goals allowed column agrees with the save percentage, but less loudly.

The goals against column points the same way. Montreal allows 3.13 goals a game, 15th of 32, and Utah allows 3.38, 23rd of 32. The honest wrinkle is that those ranks are closer together than the save percentage ranks: Montreal is 8th in team save percentage but only 15th in goals allowed, and Utah is 30th in team save percentage but 23rd in goals allowed. The team table can't tell us why those columns don't line up, so we won't pretend it can.

What it does tell us is that Montreal's edge is real but isn't a wall. A club allowing 3.13 goals a game is still conceding plenty, and Utah's offense won't be frightened by that figure. Montreal has 2 shutouts, 2nd of 32, and Utah has 1, 7th of 32, which mostly says that blanking an opponent has been scarce across the league. So the argument here isn't that Montreal shuts teams down; it's that Utah's net has been the easier target.

Both Clubs Score, So Saves Separate Them

When the attacks are this close, the crease carries more weight.

Neither club has been short of goals. Utah scores 3.56 a game, 7th of 32, and Montreal scores 3.47, 13th of 32. Shooting percentage tilts the other way, with Montreal at 12.3, 10th of 32, and Utah at 11.8, 14th of 32. Put those together and the attacks look close to a wash, which is exactly why the goaltending gap gets to be the story.

The goal differentials show how little margin either side has. Montreal is +5 overall and +0.34 a game, 12th of 32, while Utah is +3 overall and +0.18 a game, 13th of 32. Those are thin cushions for clubs that score this often, and thin cushions are where a team save percentage of .898 against a team save percentage of .870 starts to bite. If both offenses do what they usually do, the night likely turns on which crease gives up the extra goal, and the numbers say that's more likely to be Utah's.

The logic

When the attacks cancel out, the goaltending is what's left.

The Power Play Is Utah's Way Around the Gap

The strongest counterargument to Montreal's edge in goal.

Here's the best case against Montreal. Utah's power play is converting at 36.4 percent, 2nd of 32, and it's the kind of unit that can make a good team save percentage look ordinary for a night. Montreal's penalty kill is sound at 81.7 percent, 11th of 32, so this isn't a mismatch in Utah's favor on paper. It does mean Montreal's discipline matters, because every trip to the box hands the Mammoth their cleanest route past the gap in goal.

The other end of the ice is stranger. Montreal's power play is at 13 percent, 30th of 32, and Utah's penalty kill is at 70 percent, 25th of 32, so a weak unit meets a weak unit. That's a missed chance for Montreal if it stays cold, since Utah's team save percentage of .870 and a shaky kill should be an invitation. Montreal's edge in goal is strongest at even strength and in a clean game; the more this turns into a parade to the box, the more Utah's special teams can erase it.

The risk

Every trip to the box hands Utah its cleanest route past the gap in goal.

Form, Venue and the Listed Starters

What the recent results add, and what a listing can't tell us.

Jacob Fowler is listed to start for Montreal and Karel Vejmelka is listed to start for Utah. A listing isn't a certainty, and it's worth being plain about what we don't have here: the save percentages in this piece belong to the whole team, not to either goaltender. So the claim isn't that the man in the Montreal crease is better than the man in the Utah crease. The claim is that Montreal's team has kept the puck out at a higher rate, a team save percentage of .898 against a team save percentage of .870.

Form leans the same way. Montreal is 7-1-2 in its last ten and Utah is 6-4-0, though the venue offers Montreal less than you'd expect: the Canadiens are 4-2-2 at home and 5-1-1 on the road. Utah has travelled fine at 6-4-0 on the road, on top of 4-2-0 at home, so nobody should expect the building to do the work. That leaves the crease as the deciding argument, and it favors Montreal. Utah can win this, but it probably has to do it with the power play, because it hasn't shown it can win the goaltending matchup.

The call

Utah can win this, but probably not by winning the goaltending matchup.
