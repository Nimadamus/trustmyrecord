Two Defenses That Have Kept Everyone Quiet

Why this one is likely to be won a few yards at a time

Two unbeaten division rivals meet in the desert, and the number that defines them isn't the one on the scoreboard. Kansas City is allowing 16.7 points a game and Las Vegas is allowing 18, which goes a long way toward explaining how both sit at 3-0. That's a three game sample, and early season defensive rates can swing hard on one bad afternoon, so treat them as a direction rather than a verdict. Still, defenses don't stumble into numbers like that by accident.

The point differentials tell the same story from the other end: Kansas City is +38 and Las Vegas is +34. Neither team has needed a track meet to build that margin. When two defenses are this stingy, drives get shorter, punts matter more and the team that keeps starting on the better side of the field usually ends up ahead. That's the frame for everything that follows.

The edge

When both defenses hold, the game is decided by where drives begin, not by how they end.

The Raiders Defense Ends Drives by Force

A takeaway machine meets an offense that protects the ball

Las Vegas doesn't get to 18 points allowed the quiet way. The Raiders have 9 takeaways, 1st in the league, and a +5 turnover differential that also ranks 1st. Add 10 sacks, 5th in the league, and you have a defense that ends possessions on its own terms rather than waiting for a punt. Every one of those takeaways hands its own offense a short field.

Kansas City's ball security is the counterweight. The Chiefs have only 2 giveaways so far, and this is the collision that decides field position: a defense built on chaos against an offense that hasn't been coughing it up. If Las Vegas can't manufacture a turnover, it has to get off the field the hard way, snap after snap. That's a much tougher assignment against this Chiefs offense.

The edge

Las Vegas wins the field position battle with turnovers; take those away and it has to win it the long way.

Kansas City Wins Without Living in the Backfield

A patient unit facing an offense that can't run

The Chiefs get to 16.7 points allowed a different way. They have only 4 sacks, which ranks 26th, so this isn't a unit that wins by collapsing the pocket. What it does is take the ball away just often enough: 5 takeaways, 8th in the league, and a +3 turnover differential that ranks 4th. It's a defense that makes you earn every yard and waits for the mistake.

That profile suits this particular opponent. Las Vegas averages 3.2 yards a carry, 31st in the league, and 100 rushing yards a game, 19th. An offense that can't lean on the run has to throw on schedule, and a defense that doesn't need the sack can sit back, rally and tackle. Long, patient drives are exactly what the Raiders haven't shown they can build.

The edge

A Raiders offense that can't run has to string together completions against a defense content to wait it out.

Same Scoring, Very Different Routes

Kansas City moves the ball; Las Vegas gets handed it

Here's the wrinkle: both offenses score 29.3 a game, so neither defense is facing a soft touch. They just get there very differently. Kansas City gains 424 yards a game, 2nd in the league, while Las Vegas sits at 320.3, 20th. The Raiders are scoring like the Chiefs on far less yardage, which is what a league leading takeaway count does for a team's starting position.

First downs underline it. The Chiefs move the chains 22 times a game, 8th, against 19.7 for the Raiders, 18th. Kansas City flips the field with sustained drives; Las Vegas has tended to flip it with its defense. Against a Raiders unit allowing 18 a game, the Chiefs' path is to grind out long possessions and pin the hosts deep, not to trade quick scores.

The edge

Las Vegas has been scoring off short fields. Deny it those and its offense has to go the distance.

Third Down and the Third Quarter

Where a field position game finally tilts

In a game shaped by field position, third down is where possessions live or die. Kansas City converts 45.9 percent, 9th, against 41 percent for Las Vegas, 14th. Neither number is elite, but the Chiefs have the cleaner path to staying on the field, and every extra set of downs pushes the next punt a little deeper into Raiders territory.

That's where the run game earns its keep. The Chiefs run for 153.3 yards a game at 5 a carry, both 5th in the league, and that kind of ground game tends to tighten its grip after halftime, once a defense has spent a half chasing. The red zone is close to a wash, 60 percent for Kansas City against 61.5 for Las Vegas, so the scoring chances may be few and converted at similar rates. The third quarter, when Kansas City can lean on the run and the Raiders need a takeaway to change the field, is where this one tips.

The edge

Kansas City's ground game is built to win the half after halftime, and in a low scoring script that's the half that counts.
