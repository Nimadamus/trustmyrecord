One Swing Could Settle This Series

Chicago built its offense on the home run; Cleveland didn't.

This is Game 5 of a best of 5 Division Series, the series tied 2 to 2, and the cleanest way to read it is through one swing. The White Sox hit 211 home runs in the regular season, 5th in baseball, and turned that into 776 runs, which also ranked 5th. Cleveland hit 162 home runs, 27th. Those aren't two versions of the same offense; they're two different ideas about how to score.

Chicago's team OPS was .726 in the regular season, 10th, which is good but not elite. The distance between a 5th place home run total and a 10th place OPS tells you how this lineup works: it doesn't grind out long innings so much as wait for one pitch to drive. Cleveland's .696 team OPS ranked 26th, so the Guardians rarely win by out slugging anyone. If this one turns into a home run contest, only one side came built for it.

The edge

Chicago ranked 5th in home runs and 10th in OPS. That's a lineup that scores in bunches, off one swing.

Gavin Williams Misses Bats but Pays for Contact

Few baserunners, too many runs: the signature of the long ball.

Gavin Williams struck out 248 hitters over 184.1 innings in the regular season, 12.11 per nine, and opponents managed only a .213 batting average against him. His WHIP was 1.11. Yet his ERA finished at 3.76, which is the profile of a pitcher who keeps the bases fairly clean and still gives runs back in chunks. When this little traffic produces those runs, the ball leaving the yard is usually the reason.

The split matters more than the strikeouts tonight. Right-handed hitters posted a .727 OPS against Williams in the regular season, while left-handed hitters managed a .621 OPS, so the damage has come from the right side. His ERA over his last 5 starts was 2.54, but those outings were spread across a long stretch of the calendar and shouldn't be sold as a hot hand. The full sample of 184.1 innings is the truer read on how he handles a power lineup.

Chicago Has to Do It Against a Right-Hander

The power is real; the platoon split is not in Chicago's favor.

Here's the catch for the power argument. The White Sox posted a .716 OPS against right-handed pitching across 4369 plate appearances in the regular season, below their .751 OPS against lefties over 1818 plate appearances. Williams throws right-handed, so Chicago doesn't get its preferred matchup. What the White Sox do have is volume of power, and it doesn't take a big OPS to turn one mistake into a crooked number.

The season totals favor Chicago anyway. The White Sox scored 776 and allowed 720 in the regular season for a +56 run differential, while Cleveland scored 678 and allowed 667 for +11. Cleveland's 3.77 staff ERA was better than Chicago's 4.12, so the Guardians win on run prevention. The White Sox simply outscored that deficit by a wide margin, and the home run did most of the work.

The edge

+56 against +11. Chicago's run differential was built on power, Cleveland's on preventing it.

Progressive Field Has Kept These Games Small

Tight in Cleveland, loud in Chicago.

The season series went 9-8 to the White Sox, with the run tally at 73-78 across 17 meetings, about as even as a matchup gets. The two meetings at Progressive Field to open this round finished 3-0 and 4-3. Neither looks like a power showcase. Cleveland's park and Cleveland's pitching kept those games on a short leash.

Then the series moved to Chicago and the scoring broke open, with finals of 9-3 and 9-5. That's the swing Game 5 hinges on. If it plays like the first two games in Cleveland, Williams and the Guardians are comfortable. If a couple of White Sox drives carry the way they did at home, the low scoring script falls apart fast.

The pattern

At Progressive Field this series: 3-0 and 4-3. In Chicago: 9-3 and 9-5.

Sean Burke Faces a Lineup Without Thunder

The other starter doesn't carry the same risk.

The flip side of the story is that Sean Burke doesn't face the same threat. He posted a 3.34 ERA over 172.1 innings in the regular season with a .225 opponents' batting average, and he was tougher on right-handed hitters, a .646 OPS allowed, than on lefties, a .707 OPS allowed. Cleveland hit 162 home runs, 27th, and managed a .706 OPS against right-handed pitching. Burke can afford to attack the zone in a way Williams can't.

Both lineups arrive scoring. Chicago went 7-3 over its last 10 and averaged 5.3 runs a game in that stretch, while Cleveland averaged 5 runs a game over its own last 10. The difference is how the runs come. Cleveland needs sequences; Chicago needs one swing off a pitcher whose ERA has run ahead of his hit and walk numbers all year.

The edge

Burke faces a lineup that ranked 27th in home runs. Williams faces one that ranked 5th.
