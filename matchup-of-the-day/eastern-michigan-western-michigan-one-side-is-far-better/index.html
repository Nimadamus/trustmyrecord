Two Seasons Pointing in Opposite Directions

Point differential tells you who has actually been better

Western Michigan has outscored its schedule by +91. Eastern Michigan sits at -67. Records can lie a little, since a couple of coin flip finishes can move a team a game either way, but point differential is harder to fake, and these two numbers aren't close to each other. The Broncos are 7-3 and the margin says they earned it; the Eagles are 4-6 and the margin says that record might be flattering them.

Break it down per game and the picture is just as clear. Western Michigan scores 27.8 points a game and allows 18.7. Eastern Michigan scores 19.9 and allows 26.6. That's a team winning comfortably on both sides of the ball against a team that's losing on both, and when you line those up head to head, the Broncos' offense is facing a defense that gives up more than it does, while the Eagles' offense runs into a unit that has been stingier than anything it puts up.

The edge

A +91 margin against a -67 margin isn't a matchup of equals with different luck. It's one team that has been far better.

The Yardage Backs Up the Scoreboard

The margin isn't a turnover or special teams fluke

The first question to ask of any big point differential is whether the offense is actually moving the ball or living on short fields. Western Michigan averages 350.9 total yards a game, which ranks 96th nationally. That's not a juggernaut, and it's worth being honest about it, but it's real production that supports 27.8 points a game.

Eastern Michigan averages 304.4 total yards, ranked 129th. That's near the bottom of the country, and it explains why the Eagles score just 19.9 a game. When one offense is middling and the other is struggling to get out of its own way, the scoring margin isn't a mystery; it's the natural result of who can sustain drives.

The edge

Middling yardage beats bottom tier yardage, and that's most of the scoring gap in one line.

Western Michigan Wins It on the Ground

The Broncos' identity is the run, and the Eagles can't match it

The Broncos run for 184.7 yards a game, 36th nationally, and they do it efficiently at 4.7 yards a carry, which ranks 45th. That's the engine behind the margin. A team that can lean on the run controls the clock, shortens the game and keeps a weaker opponent from finding a rhythm.

Eastern Michigan's ground game is the mirror image. The Eagles run for 102.4 yards a game, ranked 129th, at 3.5 yards a carry, ranked 120th. When you can't run, every third down gets longer and every possession gets more fragile, and that's exactly the kind of team that ends a season underwater in point differential.

The edge

184.7 rushing yards a game against 102.4 is the margin in its most physical form.

Third Down Is Where Drives Live or Die

The Broncos keep the chains moving far more often

Western Michigan converts 45.4 percent of its third downs. Eastern Michigan converts 36.6 percent. That gap matters more than it looks, because a third down conversion is the difference between a field goal try or a punt and a fresh set of downs, and it compounds over a full game.

Interestingly, the raw first down counts are nearly even, 18.9 a game for the Broncos and 18.6 for the Eagles. So the separation isn't about how often either team moves the sticks in general; it's about which team does it when the drive is on the line. That's a strong argument that the Broncos' margin comes from finishing possessions, not from piling up empty first downs.

The edge

Converting 45.4 percent against 36.6 on the money down is how a good team turns similar first down totals into very different scores.

Protecting the Ball and Hitting the Quarterback

The quieter numbers lean the same way

Western Michigan has given the ball away just 8 times, which ranks 18th nationally. Eastern Michigan has 12 giveaways, ranked 55th. Both teams are on the right side of the ledger, the Broncos at +4 in turnover differential and the Eagles at +1, so this isn't a case of one side gifting games away; the Broncos are simply cleaner.

The pass rush is a sharper contrast. Western Michigan has 25 sacks, 37th in the country, while Eastern Michigan has 15, ranked 107th. A defense that can get home without blitzing makes life hard on an offense that already struggles to run, and that's a bad pairing for the Eagles.

The edge

25 sacks against 15 means the Broncos can pressure a quarterback who can't lean on his run game for help.

Where Eastern Michigan Has a Real Case

The passing volume and the road split are the Eagles' openings

To be fair to the Eagles, they actually throw for more yards than the Broncos, 202 a game against 166.2. But volume isn't efficiency: Eastern Michigan completes 61.5 percent at 5.42 yards per attempt, while Western Michigan completes 65.2 percent at 6.87. The Eagles throw because they have to, and the Broncos throw when they want to.

The situational splits don't offer much rescue either. Eastern Michigan is 2-3 on the road, and Western Michigan is 3-1 at Waldo Stadium. Penalties are a wash, 417 yards for the Broncos and 406 for the Eagles, so there's no hidden discipline edge to lean on. For the Eagles to win, they'd need the kind of game that their season long margin says they rarely play.

The edge

More passing yards on worse efficiency is a symptom of trailing, not a path to winning.

The point differential gap is wide, and the run game, third down rate and pass rush all explain where it comes from.

Why

  • Western Michigan has outscored opponents by +91 while Eastern Michigan is at -67, a gap that points to a real quality difference rather than luck.
  • The Broncos' run game produces far more yardage and efficiency than the Eagles can match, which controls the game script.

What would change it

  • If Eastern Michigan's defense holds the Broncos' run game well below its usual output, the matchup gets much tighter.
  • Multiple Western Michigan turnovers would erase the ball security edge that supports the margin.
  • An efficient passing day from the Eagles, rather than just volume, could keep them within reach.
