Two Defenses Keeping Scores Down

Why points allowed frame this whole game

Western Michigan comes in at 8-3 and Miami (OH) at 7-4, and both are still relevant this late in the year mostly because of what happens when the other team has the ball. The Broncos allow 19.5 points a game; the RedHawks allow 23.6. Neither number will make a highlight package, but in a league where weeknight shootouts break out all the time, holding opponents to the low twenties is a real skill. That's why this one projects as a game settled by field position and the third quarter, not by whichever offense gets hot first.

The point differentials show how much each defense is carrying. Western Michigan sits at +98 and Miami at +41, and most of that gap comes from what the Broncos keep off the board. The offenses are nearly identical, 28.4 points a game for Western Michigan and 27.4 for Miami. When scoring is that even, the side that concedes less usually owns the margin, and that's the Broncos' case in one line.

The edge

The offenses are almost even, so the defense that gives up less owns the margin.

Offenses That Need Short Fields

Neither attack covers much ground

Look at the yardage and the defensive story sharpens. Miami averages 363.7 total yards a game, 84th nationally, and Western Michigan averages 350, 97th. These aren't offenses that march the length of the field every possession. They need short fields, and short fields come from punts, penalties and turnovers, which is exactly where a defensive game gets decided.

Both teams lean on the run. The Broncos rush for 189.3 yards a game, 30th, at 4.8 a carry; the RedHawks rush for 166.7, 53rd, at 4.6 a carry. Passing is the afterthought, with 197 yards a game for Miami and 160.7 for Western Michigan. Run heavy offenses shorten games, fewer possessions make every stop heavier, and that tilts the night toward whoever wins the hidden yardage.

The edge

Two run first offenses mean fewer possessions, and every stop counts for more.

Penalties and Turnovers Set the Field

Where field position is actually won

Field position is where Miami has a problem it can't hide. The RedHawks have been flagged for 684 penalty yards this season, against 479 for Western Michigan. In a game likely to be played between the two defenses' comfort zones, every drive extended by a flag is one the Broncos don't have to earn. That's the cheapest way there is to lose a defensive game.

Turnovers aren't likely to bail anyone out. Western Michigan is +3 in turnover margin and Miami is +2, and neither defense takes the ball away often: 12 takeaways for the Broncos, 102nd nationally, and 10 for the RedHawks, 122nd. What both do well is protect it, with Miami giving it away just 8 times, 11th, and Western Michigan 9 times, 18th. So the short field probably won't come from a pick six; it's more likely to come from a punt that flips the field or a flag that keeps a drive alive.

The edge

Both teams protect the ball, so the cleaner penalty sheet is the real field position weapon.

Third Down Decides Who Punts From Where

The conversion gap shapes every possession

The third down split best captures how this game should flow. Western Michigan converts 45.1 percent of its third downs; Miami converts 33.8 percent. In a low possession game, that's the difference between a drive that flips the field and a punt from your own end. Keep the chains moving and you dictate where the other offense starts.

Accuracy explains a lot of it. The Broncos complete 65.3 percent of their passes, 39th nationally, while the RedHawks complete 56.4 percent, 120th. Oddly, Miami moves the chains about as often overall, 19.5 first downs a game against 19.1 for Western Michigan, which tells you the RedHawks get theirs on early downs and on the ground. The trouble starts when they fall behind schedule, and a defense playing at home knows that's the moment to pin its ears back.

The edge

Miami is fine on early downs; the danger is every third and long.

The Pass Rush and the Third Quarter

Where a game like this usually turns

The pass rush gives Western Michigan another lever in those long yardage spots. The Broncos have 28 sacks, 32nd nationally, against 24 for Miami, 58th. Put a quarterback who's already fighting accuracy issues into third and long against that front, and the drive usually ends with a punt. That's how a defense manufactures field position without forcing a single turnover.

Venue matters too. Western Michigan is 4-1 at home, while Miami is 3-2 on the road after going 4-2 at home. The third quarter is where defensive games like this one tend to turn: adjustments land, the run game either wears on a front or doesn't, and one flipped possession sets the field for the rest of the night. The Broncos carry the better tools for that stretch, and they get to use them at Waldo Stadium.

The edge

One flipped possession after halftime can set the field for the whole night.
