The Gap Between the Pipes

Two team save percentages from last season, side by side.

Start with the number that separates these clubs. Last season the Mammoth stopped pucks at a .893 team save percentage, while the Oilers finished at a .883 team save percentage. That looks like a rounding error until you remember how many shots a season holds. The league table ranked Utah 19th of 32 and Edmonton 29th of 32 on that measure.

None of this is a verdict on one goalie. Tristan Jarry is listed to start for Edmonton and Karel Vejmelka is listed to start for Utah, but the save percentage in this brief belongs to each whole team last season. It says more about the goaltending room and the defence in front of it than about the name on the lineup card. Still, a club that sat 29th of 32 last season has something to prove before anyone trusts it to protect a lead.

The edge

Last season the Mammoth stopped more pucks, and the table put Edmonton near the bottom.

Where Edmonton Leaked

Goals allowed and clean sheets from last season.

Last season the Oilers allowed 3.28 goals a game, which ranked 25th of 32. Their .883 team save percentage sat 29th of 32, so the leak showed up in the results and not just in the ratio. They did record 5 shutouts last season, 6th of 32, which tells you clean sheets happened. They were the exception.

The offence is what kept Edmonton afloat. The Oilers scored 3.44 goals a game last season and went 41-30-11, good for 93 standings points. Scoring covered for the goaltending, but that only works while the shots keep going in. Against a team that stopped more of them last season, the cushion gets thinner.

The leak

Edmonton's scoring covered for its goaltending last season, and that is a fragile way to win.

Utah's Higher Floor

What the Mammoth back end gave them last season.

Utah's floor was higher last season. The Mammoth allowed 2.93 goals a game, 10th of 32, behind a .893 team save percentage that ranked 19th of 32. Neither figure is elite, and that is the point. A middling back end is enough when the opponent's sits near the bottom of the table.

Last season the Mammoth went 43-33-6, collected 92 standings points and finished with a goal differential of +28. They recorded only 3 shutouts, 15th of 32, so they were not winning by locking games down. They were winning by keeping the damage manageable, which is a quieter but more repeatable habit.

The floor

Utah was never elite in goal last season, only steadier than Edmonton.

Edmonton's Best Answer

Scoring and special teams, from last season.

Edmonton's best answer is the attack. Last season the Oilers scored 3.44 goals a game, 6th of 32, and their power play converted at 30.6 percent, 1st of 32. Utah's defence allowed 2.93 goals a game last season, 10th of 32. That is a real test, but it is not a wall.

Special teams add a wrinkle. Utah's penalty kill ran at 78.1 percent last season, 19th of 32, so the Oilers power play has a soft target. Edmonton's own penalty kill was 77.8 percent, 20th of 32. Both clubs were vulnerable shorthanded last season, which puts more weight on whoever stops the puck at even strength.

The counter

The Oilers power play led the league last season, and it is their cleanest path around the goaltending gap.

Early Form and the Home Edge

A small sample, a venue, and a modest tilt.

The early results agree, with a caveat. Edmonton is 5-1-1 this season after seven games and Utah is 6-2-0 after eight. Those samples are tiny. Last season's team save percentages, .883 for Edmonton and .893 for Utah, remain the better guide to how this game breaks.

Utah went 22-16-3 at home last season, while Edmonton went 19-16-6 on the road. The gap is modest, but it leans toward the home side, and this game is at the Delta Center. When the goaltending numbers and the venue point the same way, the lean is worth respecting without pretending it is large.

The tilt

Every last season split leans a little toward Utah, and none of them leans far.
