The Volume Is Real, and So Is the Efficiency

Cincinnati leads the league in passing yards and does it without wasting throws.

Cincinnati throws for 298.9 yards a game, 1st in the league, and the easy suspicion with a number like that is that it's padded by a few shootouts. The efficiency says otherwise. The Bengals average 7.56 yards per pass attempt and complete 74.7 percent of their throws, and that completion rate also ranks 1st. Volume built on bad throws collapses against a decent defense; volume built on accuracy just keeps showing up.

Total offense tells the same story from another angle. The Bengals gain 395.3 yards a game, 6th in the league, and the air carries most of that load. That's useful for Washington in one narrow sense: there's no mystery about where the damage comes from. The problem is that knowing it and stopping it are different things, and nobody this season has managed to make this passing game look ordinary for long.

The edge

First in passing yards and first in completion rate is a rare pairing. Volume and accuracy don't usually live in the same offense.

A Passing Offense Because the Run Doesn't Work

Cincinnati's weak rushing explains the air totals, and defenses know it.

The flip side of the league's best passing attack is a running game that barely registers. Cincinnati runs for 84.8 yards a game, 30th, at 3.9 yards a carry, 23rd. That's the context for the 298.9 passing yards: this isn't a team throwing to chase games, not with a 7-2 record and a +85 margin. It throws because throwing is what works, and it has decided not to pretend otherwise.

Washington's staff will see the same thing. When the run poses little threat, a defense can drop extra bodies into coverage and dare the offense to grind out short gains. The catch is that a team completing 74.7 percent of its passes is perfectly happy grinding out short gains through the air. Selling out against the pass only works if the quarterback gets impatient, and nothing in these numbers suggests he does.

The tension

Daring Cincinnati to be patient is a strange plan against an offense whose whole identity is patient, accurate throws.

Washington Can't Match It Through the Air

The gap per throw is wide, and the pass rush is unlikely to close it.

Washington's own passing game sits squarely in the middle of the pack, and below it on efficiency. The Commanders throw for 210.2 yards a game, 19th, at 5.91 yards per attempt, 21st, and complete 62.5 percent of their passes, also 21st. Set 7.56 a throw against 5.91 and the gap is the whole game in miniature: one side gains real yardage every time it drops back, the other has to work for it. Washington can't win a game where both quarterbacks trade throws.

Our dataset has no clean figure for Washington's pass defense, so pressure is the best proxy available, and it isn't encouraging. Washington has 18 sacks, 26th, against Cincinnati's 26, 14th. The Commanders' more realistic path is the ground: 116.8 rushing yards a game and 4.3 a carry, both 11th. Long, clock eating drives keep the Bengals' passing game on the sideline, and that's the only kind of pass defense that never misses a tackle.

The gap

Washington's best answer to Cincinnati's arm may be its own running game, keeping the ball away from it entirely.

Where the Passing Game Turns Into Points

Third downs and red zone trips are where the air attack pays off.

Accurate passing shows up most clearly on third down, and that's where the split in this matchup is starkest. Cincinnati converts 51.9 percent of its third downs, 1st in the league. Washington converts 33.3 percent, 32nd. An offense that completes 74.7 percent of its throws doesn't need third and short to stay on the field; it can convert third and seven with a throw that most teams treat as a coin flip.

The same pattern carries inside the 20. The Bengals turn 71.1 percent of red zone trips into touchdowns, 3rd, while Washington sits at 54.6 percent, 20th. Add the first downs, 23.2 a game for Cincinnati against 18.2 for Washington, and the picture is consistent. The Bengals stay on the field longer, reach scoring range more often and finish more of those trips with seven instead of three.

The finish

The best third down offense against the worst one. That's the passing edge expressed as drives kept alive.

The Margin Favors Cincinnati, With One Real Catch

Washington protects the ball well, and that's the only lever it really has.

The season margins line up with everything above. Cincinnati is +85, scoring 30.9 points a game and allowing 21.4; Washington is -27, scoring 21.8 and allowing 24.8. The Bengals are 4-1 on the road, and Washington is 2-2-1 at home, so there isn't much of a venue argument to lean on either. A passing game this productive is portable, which is part of why the road record looks the way it does.

The one place Washington genuinely wins is the turnover column. The Commanders are +7, 2nd in the league, with only 7 giveaways and 14 takeaways, 10th. Cincinnati is +2, 14th, with 10 giveaways. Throwing as often as the Bengals do creates more chances for a tipped ball or a jumped route, and Washington's path to an upset almost certainly runs through one or two of those. It's a real threat, but it's a hope rather than a plan.

The caveat

Washington's ball security is legitimate. It just has to produce a short field or two against an offense that rarely gives one away cheaply.

Confidence · Strong

The league's most productive and most accurate passing game draws a Washington team that can't match it through the air and struggles to pressure the quarterback.

Why

  • Cincinnati leads the league in both passing yards and completion rate, so the volume rests on efficiency rather than garbage time.
  • The third down gap, best in the league against worst, turns that passing edge directly into extra possessions and points.
  • Washington's weak pass rush and middling passing offense leave it few ways to slow the Bengals or keep pace with them.

What would change it

  • Washington wins the turnover battle by two or more, using its ball security to steal short fields.
  • The Commanders' running game controls the clock and limits Cincinnati's possessions.
  • Washington's pass rush outperforms its season profile and forces the Bengals' quarterback off schedule.
