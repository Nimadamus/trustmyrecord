Last Season's Goaltending Gap

Team save percentage, last season

Last season the Minnesota Wild finished with a .908 team save percentage, 2nd of 32 in the league. The Nashville Predators finished with a .897 team save percentage, 14th of 32. That's a club near the top of the table against one parked in the middle, and it's the cleanest separation between these two teams on paper. Both clubs open this season at 0-0-0, so last season's numbers are the only honest read we have.

Jesper Wallstedt is listed to start for Minnesota and Juuse Saros is listed to start for Nashville. Neither listing comes with a goalie number here, and that's worth being clear about. The .908 and .897 figures from last season are team save percentages, which means they reflect the skaters clearing the crease as much as the man standing in it. What they tell you is which organization, top to bottom, kept the puck out of its own net more reliably last season.

Where the Saves Showed Up Last Season

Goals allowed and shutouts, last season

A save percentage gap only matters if it moves the scoreboard, and last season it did. Minnesota allowed 2.93 goals a game, 4th of 32. Nashville allowed 3.28 goals a game, 26th of 32, which put the Predators in the bottom tier of the league at keeping the puck out. The direction matches the save percentage split exactly.

The shutout column makes the same point with less nuance. The Wild recorded 8 shutouts last season, 2nd of 32, while the Predators recorded 1, which ranked 26th of 32. Shutouts are a blunt instrument and a small count, but eight nights of zero against one tells you which team could lock a game down in its own end. That pattern held across the whole of last season, not a hot month.

Special Teams Don't Explain It

Power play and penalty kill, last season

Here's the honest counterpoint for Nashville. Last season the Predators killed penalties at 81.7 percent, 5th of 32, while Minnesota's penalty kill ran at 79.8 percent, 16th of 32. So whatever separated these two teams in goal last season, it wasn't the Predators bleeding goals while shorthanded; their kill was the better unit.

The other side of special teams leans Minnesota. The Wild's power play converted 25.2 percent last season, 3rd of 32, against 23.1 percent and 11th of 32 for Nashville. Put the two together and special teams look close to a wash, which pushes the explanation for the goals against gap back toward the save percentage itself. That's the uncomfortable read for Nashville: the problem last season lived in how many pucks got through, not in the penalty box.

The Margin Last Season

Goal differential and scoring, last season

Last season Minnesota outscored opponents by +0.39 goals a game, 6th of 32, and finished at +32 overall. Nashville went the other way, -0.27 a game and -22 overall, 23rd of 32. Those are the numbers that decide standings, and the save percentage gap is a big part of how they got built.

The attack was a gap too, just a narrower one in league terms. The Wild scored 3.32 goals a game last season, 10th of 32, and the Predators scored 3.01, 20th of 32. Shooting percentage told a similar story: 11.2 percent for Minnesota, 15th, and 10.6 percent for Nashville, 23rd. Minnesota ranked 4th in goals allowed and 10th in goals scored last season, so its edge was built more on the back end than the front.

Records Last Season and the Trip In

Records, splits and the closing stretch, last season

Last season the Wild went 46-24-12 for 104 standings points, and the Predators went 38-34-10 for 86. Minnesota travelled well, going 23-14-4 on the road last season. Nashville was 21-17-3 at home, a winning record in its own building but hardly a fortress.

The closing stretch last season split the same way. Minnesota finished 6-4-0 over its last ten games and Nashville went 4-5-1. None of that carries into this season automatically, with both teams at 0-0-0, but a club that ranked 2nd of 32 in team save percentage last season is the safer bet to keep that structure than one that ranked 14th.
