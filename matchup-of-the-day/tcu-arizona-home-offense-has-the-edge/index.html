Something Has to Give

Arizona's 34.9 points a game meets a defense allowing 14.1

Arizona scores 34.9 points a game. TCU gives up 14.1. That's a gap of more than a touchdown, and it's why this game earned the slot: one of those numbers has to move, and the read here is that the home offense moves it further than the visiting defense does.

TCU's record undersells the unit Arizona has to solve. The Horned Frogs are 4-4 with a +73 point differential, an odd pairing, and this data doesn't show game margins, so we can't say how those losses happened. What it does say is that a team allowing 14.1 and scoring 23.3 has a weaker half, and it isn't the defense. Arizona, at 6-2 and +128 while allowing 18.9, has had the better offense and the better record, though not the better defense.

The edge

One of these numbers has to move, 34.9 scored or 14.1 allowed, and the home offense is the likelier mover.

The Volume Is Real, and So Is the Efficiency

324.1 passing yards a game at 8.45 yards an attempt

The Wildcats throw for 324.1 yards a game, 5th of 136 teams, and that isn't just a team passing a lot because it has to. They average 8.45 yards an attempt, 21st, while completing 73.7 percent of their throws, 3rd nationally. Plenty of offenses are accurate because they throw short, and plenty are explosive because they accept incompletions. Arizona is doing a good version of both, which is the hard thing to scheme away.

The run game is another story. At 131.5 yards a game, 105th, and 4 yards a carry, 94th, this isn't a balanced attack, and TCU will know exactly where the threat is. That's the honest knock on the Arizona case: a defense this stingy can commit to the pass without much fear of being punished on the ground. Even so, the Wildcats have piled up 455.6 total yards a game, 16th, with everyone they've played knowing the same thing.

The edge

Completing 73.7 percent at 8.45 yards an attempt means Arizona isn't trading accuracy for depth.

Third Down Is Where a Stingy Defense Gets Tested

Arizona converts 51.9 percent, TCU's offense 42.7

Arizona converts 51.9 percent of its third downs, and TCU's offense converts 42.7. Be clear about what that comparison is: two offenses, not an offense against the defense across from it, because we don't have TCU's third down rate allowed. It still matters. A defense allowing 14.1 points a game doesn't hand out quick scores, so beating it usually means long drives, and long drives run through third down.

Arizona averages 23.1 first downs a game to TCU's 21.3, a narrower margin than the third down rates would suggest. That's the quiet argument for the Horned Frogs, who do move the chains. But a 73.7 percent completion rate keeps the Wildcats out of the long yardage spots where good defenses make their living. If Arizona stays on schedule into the second half, the third quarter is when that pressure tends to show up on the scoreboard.

The edge

Converting 51.9 percent of third downs is how you grind on a defense that won't give up the quick score.

TCU's Way Back In Is the Football

14 takeaways against an offense that protects the ball

If this defense flips the matchup, it does it with takeaways. TCU has 14 of them, 21st nationally, and a short field is the cleanest way for an offense like this one to score. The trouble is that Arizona hasn't been generous: 8 giveaways, 31st, on the way to a +4 turnover margin that ranks 19th. TCU sits at +1, so the Horned Frogs' ball hawking has mostly gone to cover their own mistakes.

The pass rush is the other lever, and it's less convincing. TCU's 18 sacks rank 55th, which is fine but not the sort of pressure that rattles a quarterback group completing 73.7 percent. There's also a real hole in what we can say here, since we don't have a usable yards allowed figure for either defense and nothing in this data adjusts for schedule. Points allowed is carrying the defensive side of the argument, so treat TCU's number as strong rather than fully explained.

The risk

TCU's 14 takeaways are the live threat, but Arizona's 8 giveaways say that door doesn't open often.

The Other Half of the Problem Is TCU's Offense

3.5 yards a carry leaves the Horned Frogs throwing

Even if TCU's defense holds up, somebody has to score. The Horned Frogs run for 114.6 yards a game, 120th, at 3.5 a carry, 122nd, so they're going to throw. They can do that, at 275.9 passing yards a game, 33rd, but 7.58 yards an attempt ranks 54th and the total offense lands at 390.5 yards, 64th. That passing game now has to work against an Arizona defense with 25 sacks, 12th nationally.

This is where the secondary story earns its place. Neither defense has been giving much up, 14.1 and 18.9 points a game, so field position matters, and TCU is the side more likely to lose it. The Horned Frogs have 13 giveaways, 108th, and 541 penalty yards to Arizona's 446. An offense scoring 23.3 a game doesn't have much room to give those yards back.

The edge

At 3.5 yards a carry, TCU has to throw into a defense with 25 sacks.

Why the Lean Stays With the Home Offense

A 4-0 home record and a passing game built to travel the field

Arizona is 4-0 at home and 2-2 on the road, which is a small sample but a clear one. TCU is 1-1 on the road, and that tells you almost nothing either way. We also can't split the 34.9 by venue from this data, so the home record is supporting evidence rather than proof that the offense is better in this building.

Both headline numbers probably give a little. Arizona likely doesn't reach its 34.9 against this defense, and TCU likely allows more than its 14.1. The question is which side gives more, and an offense throwing for 324.1 yards a game, at home, against a defense with a middling pass rush, is the better bet to impose itself. TCU has the best single unit on the field, but Arizona has the more complete team.

The lean

Both numbers give a little, but the 14.1 is the one more likely to break.
