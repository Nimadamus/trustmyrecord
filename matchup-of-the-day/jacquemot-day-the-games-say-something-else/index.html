The Games Say Something Else

The unit that actually gets played tells a cleaner story than either win loss line.

Elsa Jacquemot is 8-24 this season. Kayla Day is 15-12. Those two lines read like a mismatch, and then you check the list and find Jacquemot at No. 114 and Day at No. 117, which is nothing at all. Match records are blunt instruments: they count a tight loss and a rout identically, and they hand out credit for whoever drew the kinder half of a draw.

So drop a level. Day has won 53.1% of the games she has played this season; Jacquemot has won 42.5% of hers. In raw counts that is 302-267 for Day, a differential of +35, against 291-393 for Jacquemot, a differential of -102. A win loss record can flatter a player or bury one, but nobody accumulates a -102 across a full season by bad luck.

The edge

53.1% of games against 42.5% is not a rounding gap, it is the match.

The Rankings Agree, The Sets Do Not

Three ranking spots and 43 points separate two players the scoreboard does not.

Jacquemot carries 668 ranking points at No. 114, one place below the No. 113 she held a week ago. Day sits at No. 117 with 625 points, exactly where she sat a week ago. By the only measure the tour publishes every Monday, these are interchangeable players. Everything underneath it says otherwise.

Sets tell the same story as games, only louder. Day is 35-26 in sets this season, a 57.4% set win rate, while Jacquemot is 21-52 at 28.8%. Neither has been feasting in main draws, Day at 5-9 and Jacquemot at 6-17, which is why the points sit where they do. But losing main draw matches is a different thing from being outplayed inside them, and 28.8% of sets is the figure that should worry anybody backing Jacquemot.

The gap

Day has won 57.4% of her sets this season. Jacquemot has won 28.8%. Same ranking neighbourhood, different sport.

When Day Wins, She Wins Early

The games gap shows up in the scoreline, and so does its one weakness.

Here is the shape of Day's 15-12: 13 of those 15 wins arrived in straight sets, 86.7% of them. Jacquemot's split from her 8-24 is 4 of 8, an even 50%. That is exactly what a games gap looks like once it reaches a scoreboard. A player winning 53.1% of games closes in two; a player at 42.5% usually has to survive a third to win anything.

The other side of it is real and it is Jacquemot's whole case. Day is 2-5 in matches that went to a deciding set this season, while Jacquemot is 4-5, so the longer this runs the thinner Day's advantage gets. Day has been 3-2 in tiebreaks, a sample far too small to lean on either way; Jacquemot is 3-7, a 30% return that at least covers a season. Win in two or take your chances is not a bad summary of Day's year.

The pattern

86.7% of Day's wins have come in straight sets. She is 2-5 when the match refuses to end there.

Ten Matches Say One Thing, The Season Says Another

Why the last ten results are the least useful thing on this page.

Recent form flattens all of this. Day is 5-5 over her last ten matches, Jacquemot 4-6, and if the two strips were all you had you would call the match a coin flip. Plenty of previews will do precisely that. Ten results is ten results, though, and Jacquemot's 4-6 sits on top of a 25% win rate for the year.

The games measure does not have that problem. It is built off every game of 291-393 for Jacquemot and 302-267 for Day, which is why 42.5% against 53.1% survives scrutiny that a ten match strip cannot. Jacquemot has also played 25 tournaments to Day's 13, so hers is the larger body of evidence and it still reads worse. Form is the noise here. The season is the signal.

The caution

A 5-5 and a 4-6 over ten matches will tempt you. A season of 291-393 against 302-267 should not.

Round 2 in Barranquilla

Both have already won at this event, and one of them has a season behind it.

Both arrive in Round 2 having already banked wins in Barranquilla: Jacquemot is 2-0 at the Kia Open this year, Day 1-0. Campo 8, best of 3 sets. Two wins on site beats one, and neither is heavy evidence next to what a full season of games has already established. Match sharpness is a real thing; it is not worth ten points of game win rate.

The route

Jacquemot needs a third set. Day, at 53.1% of games won, needs the match to behave normally.
