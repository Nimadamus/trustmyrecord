Strength Meets Strength

The best unit on each sideline is pointed straight at the other.

Both teams arrive at 6-1, and they got there in opposite ways. Buffalo scores 33.3 points a game, while Minnesota allows just 13.3, so the best unit on each sideline is aimed squarely at the other. The distance between those figures is more than a touchdown, and only one of them leaves this building intact.

The Bills' production isn't a scheduling fluke, either. They lead the league at 404.6 total yards a game, 1st of 32 teams, and their +52 point differential has come despite a defense that's been far from airtight. Minnesota's +47 says the Vikings have been every bit as dominant, just through suffocation rather than volume. It's 7 games of evidence for each side, enough to describe a team and not quite enough to crown one.

The edge

Buffalo scores 33.3 a game; Minnesota allows 13.3. One of those numbers is about to move.

Balance a Defense Can't Sit On

Buffalo wins on the ground and through the air, efficiently.

What makes Buffalo so hard to defend is that nothing about the attack is one dimensional. The Bills run for 148.4 yards a game, 2nd in the league, at 4.8 yards a carry, which ranks 4th. That's not a team grinding out volume on poor efficiency; it's a ground game that genuinely wins snaps. Load the box to stop it and you're left dealing with the passing game behind it.

And that passing game is efficient rather than merely busy. Buffalo averages 7.86 yards per pass attempt, 3rd in the league, even though its raw volume of 244.7 passing yards a game only ranks 11th. A 65.9 completion percentage says those throws are landing, too. Minnesota's best answer is pressure, and 25 sacks, 8th in the league, prove the Vikings can get home; the trouble is that an offense averaging 4.8 a carry rarely has to drop back on predictable downs.

The edge

The Bills average 7.86 yards per pass attempt behind a run game good enough that nobody can simply tee off on the quarterback.

Staying on the Field

Buffalo attacks the exact place Minnesota's defense earns its keep.

Third down is where a great defense usually makes its living, and it's where Buffalo has been sharpest. The Bills convert 44.2 percent of third downs, 6th in the league. That matters here precisely because Minnesota's 13.3 points allowed is built on getting off the field; a defense that can't win the money down loses its main weapon.

The other side of the ledger compounds it. Minnesota's offense converts just 36 percent of its own third downs, 25th in the league, and averages 15.6 first downs a game against Buffalo's 23.9. Short Vikings possessions mean more snaps for their defense, and more snaps against an offense this balanced is how a stout unit starts to sag late. It's an early season sample, but the shape of it is consistent.

The edge

Buffalo moves the chains on 44.2 percent of third downs; Minnesota's own offense converts 36. That leaves the Vikings defense on the field far too long.

Why Minnesota Can't Simply Outscore Them

Buffalo finishes drives, and the Vikings aren't built to chase.

If Buffalo reaches the red zone, the numbers say it finishes. The Bills turn 70 percent of red zone trips into touchdowns, 5th in the league, while Minnesota converts 56.3 percent, 19th. In a game where both defenses could force field goal tries, that gap is the difference between a scoreboard that drifts and one that jumps.

The honest counterpoint is Buffalo's defense, which allows 25.9 points a game and is the obvious soft spot on this card. But Minnesota is poorly built to exploit it. The Vikings score 20 a game on 299.3 total yards, 28th in the league, and throw for just 165.6 yards a game, 30th, at 5.42 yards per pass attempt. An offense that limited needs its defense to hold Buffalo well under its usual output, which is exactly what this matchup argues against.

The edge

Minnesota throws for 165.6 yards a game. That's not an offense built to chase.

The Case for Minnesota

Turnovers and discipline are the clearest way to flip this.

The Vikings' path runs through the turnover column. Minnesota is +7 in turnover margin, 3rd in the league, built on 12 takeaways and only 5 giveaways. Buffalo is -2, 21st, having given the ball away 11 times. If this game swings, it swings there, because a short field is the one thing that lets a 28th ranked yardage offense score without needing to drive.

Venue, oddly, is a wash. Minnesota is 3-0 at home, but Buffalo is 3-0 on the road, so neither side has blinked away from comfort yet. Discipline is the quieter factor: the Bills have 526 penalty yards against Minnesota's 314, and free yards are how a defense like this one gets off the field without making a play. Turnover margin is also among the noisiest stats in football over 7 games, which is why it reads better as a risk than as a foundation.

The risk

Minnesota is +7 in turnover margin and Buffalo is -2. That's the Vikings' clearest route through this matchup.

Buffalo Bills

Confidence · Moderate

Buffalo's balance and finishing should crack a great defense more often than Minnesota's limited offense can keep pace.

Why

  • The Bills score 33.3 a game and lead the league in total yards with both the run and the pass working.
  • Buffalo converts 44.2 percent on third down, attacking the very way Minnesota's defense gets off the field.
  • Minnesota's passing game ranks 30th, so it can't comfortably win a higher scoring game.

What would change it

  • Buffalo giveaways feeding Minnesota's +7 turnover margin with short fields.
  • Vikings pressure forcing the Bills into a pass heavy, predictable script early.
  • Penalties extending Minnesota drives and stalling Buffalo's.
