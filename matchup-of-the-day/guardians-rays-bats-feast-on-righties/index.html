Tampa Bay's Lineup Changes Shape Against Right-Handers

The Rays are a different offense depending on which arm is throwing

Tampa Bay leads this League Championship Series 3 to 2, and Game 6 at Tropicana Field turns on a split that shaped the Rays' lineup all year. In the regular season they put up a .752 OPS against right-handed pitching over 4134 plate appearances, and a .679 OPS against left-handers over 1972. That gap rests on thousands of plate appearances on each side, so it isn't a hot week dressed up as a trend. It describes how this offense is built.

Their overall .728 OPS ranked 8th in the regular season, which is already better than the Rays usually get credit for. The blended number hides the actual profile, though. Against righties this lineup played well above that ranking, and against lefties it looked like an ordinary club. Cleveland sending a right-hander out for Game 6 is the exact matchup Tampa Bay would have picked.

The edge

Against right-handers, Tampa Bay's .752 OPS plays like an elite offense; against lefties, its .679 OPS doesn't.

Shawn Armstrong Is the Wrong Arm for This Lineup

A reliever's line, a thin sample, and a split that leaks to righties

Shawn Armstrong takes the ball without a regular season start to his name. His 35.1 innings all came in relief, so read his line as a sketch, not a verdict. In the regular season he had a 4.58 ERA and a 1.39 WHIP, with 8.66 strikeouts per nine undercut by 4.84 walks per nine. Opponents batted .229 against him, so the damage didn't come from a flood of hits. It came from traffic he put on base himself.

The split points the wrong way for Cleveland. Right-handed hitters posted a .748 OPS against Armstrong, compared with a .657 OPS from left-handers, and the sample behind both figures is small. Whatever lineup Tampa Bay writes out, it comes from a team that produced a .752 OPS against right-handed pitching in the regular season. If Armstrong's command drifts the way his walk rate suggests it can, the Rays are exactly the opponent that makes it hurt.

The risk

Armstrong allowed 4.84 walks per nine in the regular season, and free baserunners are the last thing to hand this lineup.

Cleveland's Bats Don't Have a Counterpunch of the Same Size

A bottom tier offense with a mild split of its own

The Guardians finished the regular season with a .696 OPS, 26th in the majors, and ranked 24th in runs and 27th in home runs. Their own platoon split is modest: a .706 OPS against right-handers and a .675 OPS against left-handers. Nothing on that sheet suggests Cleveland can match a big Tampa Bay inning swing for swing. They need to win this one a different way.

Freddy Peralta at least offers a way in. In the regular season he allowed a .757 OPS to left-handed hitters and a .688 OPS to right-handed hitters, so Cleveland's lefty bats are where the pressure has to come from. His full line, a 4.42 ERA and a 1.33 WHIP over 169.0 innings, is a more honest read than the 0.82 ERA from his last four starts. Those starts were spread far apart on the calendar and shouldn't be mistaken for current form.

The opening

Peralta gave up a .757 OPS to left-handed hitters in the regular season; that's the only crack Cleveland has to work with.

The Season Series Has Leaned Hard Toward Tampa Bay

Eleven meetings, and the runs tell a lopsided story

Across 11 meetings this season Cleveland is 3-8 against Tampa Bay, and the runs read 23-38. That's not a rivalry being decided at the margins. The Rays have been the better team in this pairing most of the time, and the Guardians have rarely gotten their offense going against them.

This series has been closer than the full record, which is how Tampa Bay finds itself up 3 to 2 rather than already through. Tight, low scoring games are where a platoon edge counts most, because a single extra baserunner against the wrong-handed pitcher can decide the night. Game 6 sets up that way again, with a right-hander on the mound for Cleveland.

The pattern

The Run Differential Backs the Platoon Case, and the Bullpen Is Cleveland's Hope

Tampa Bay's season margin dwarfs Cleveland's, but the relief corps tilts the other way

The secondary evidence supports the main case. Tampa Bay finished the regular season with a +86 run differential, 736 runs scored against 650 allowed, while Cleveland ended at +11 on 678 scored and 667 allowed. The Rays also went 55-26 at home. Put the run margin next to the split and you get a team that was genuinely good and especially dangerous in the matchup it gets tonight.

Cleveland's real advantage is in the bullpen: a 3.12 bullpen ERA and 10.55 strikeouts per nine in the regular season, against 3.42 and 8.92 for Tampa Bay. If Armstrong's outing is short, the Guardians can go to relievers who miss more bats. Still, the game runs through Tampa Bay's first look at a right-hander, and the regular season says that's where the Rays do their best work. Cleveland's plan only works if Armstrong gets through that stretch without much damage.

The counter

Cleveland's 3.12 bullpen ERA in the regular season is the one place the Guardians look like the stronger side.
