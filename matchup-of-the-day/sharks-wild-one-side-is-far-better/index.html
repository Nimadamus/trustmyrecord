The Margin Is the Whole Story

Goal differential per game, and why it deserves belief this season

Through 17 games each, the Wild outscore opponents by 1.05 goals a game and the Sharks get outscored by a full goal, a differential of -1 per game. In season totals that's +18 for Minnesota and -17 for San Jose. Hockey games get decided by bounces all the time, so a club winning its average night by more than a goal isn't living on luck. The league table agrees: the Wild are 4th of 32 in differential per game and the Sharks are 28th of 32.

The records back up the margin, which isn't always true this early in a season. Minnesota is 11-3-3 with 25 standings points, and San Jose is 7-9-1 with 15. When a record and a goal differential tell the same story, you've got less reason to suspect either one. A team can flatter its record by stealing close games, but it's much harder to fake a differential of this size over 17 games.

Where the Margin Actually Comes From

Goals for and goals against, split apart

Split the margin in half and the goals against column does most of the talking. The Wild allow 2.24 goals a game, 1st of 32, while the Sharks allow 3.59, 26th of 32. That's the widest line on the sheet, and it's where Minnesota has built its season.

The offense matters less than you'd guess from the differential. Minnesota scores 3.29 goals a game, 13th of 32, which is solid without being frightening. San Jose scores 2.59, 29th of 32, so the Sharks are short in the exact place they'd need to be strong to keep pace. They don't score enough to cover what they give up, and the club across from them gives up less than anyone in the league.

Special Teams Don't Explain It

The one part of the sheet where these clubs look alike

You'd expect a gulf this size to show up on the special teams, and it mostly doesn't. The Wild's power play runs at 25.4 percent, 8th of 32, and the Sharks' runs at 23 percent, 10th of 32. On the penalty kill San Jose is actually a hair ahead, 81 percent and 11th of 32 against Minnesota's 80.4 percent and 12th of 32. It's the Sharks' best argument for competing tonight, and it's a legitimate one.

These percentages can't tell us how often each club gets a power play, so there's a limit to how far this goes. Still, with the rates this close, the margin looks like it's being built somewhere else. It doesn't look like a finishing fluke either: Minnesota's shooting percentage is 10.9, 22nd of 32, so the Wild aren't riding a hot stick. San Jose's 9.2 shooting percentage, 30th of 32, is low enough that some of its scoring trouble could ease on its own, though that's a hope and not a measurement.

The catch

The rates are close, and the margin isn't. That gap has to be coming from somewhere else.

In Goal, the Numbers Line Up for One Side

Team save percentage, and who is listed to start

Minnesota carries a .911 team save percentage, 2nd of 32, and San Jose carries a .892 team save percentage, 14th of 32. Those are whole team figures, not any one goalie's. Jesper Wallstedt is listed to start for the Wild and Yaroslav Askarov is listed to start for the Sharks. A listing is all that is until the game begins, so the argument here rests on the clubs and not on the names.

San Jose's rank is the odd piece of this. A team save percentage sitting 14th of 32 doesn't fit neatly beside goals allowed ranked 26th of 32, and nothing in this data explains the difference, so we won't guess at it. Minnesota's marks do fit together: 2nd of 32 in team save percentage and 1st of 32 in goals allowed. That agreement is why the Wild's defensive standing looks sturdy instead of lucky.

In goal

The Building and the Form Point the Same Way

Home and road records, plus the last ten

The venue doesn't do San Jose any favors. The Wild are 4-0-1 at home, without a regulation loss in their own building. The Sharks are 4-4-1 on the road, which is actually better than their 3-5-0 at home, so travel isn't what's hurting them. Home records this small don't prove much alone, but Minnesota's 7-3-2 road mark says the Wild aren't just a home ice story anyway.

Recent form runs the same direction, with Minnesota 7-1-2 over its last ten and San Jose 3-6-1. A last ten sample is noisy and the argument here doesn't lean on it, but it does remove the easy rebuttal that the season numbers have gone stale. A single game can ignore a margin, and San Jose needs this to be one of those nights. After 17 games of evidence, the Wild aren't a team that makes those nights easy to find.

Why

What would change it

  • A change to the listed starters before puck drop would make the team save percentage comparison a weaker guide.
  • San Jose's penalty kill and power play are close to Minnesota's, so a game decided mostly on special teams would narrow the gap.
  • If San Jose's 9.2 shooting percentage, 30th of 32, climbs on the night, the Sharks' scoring problem eases and the margin matters less.
