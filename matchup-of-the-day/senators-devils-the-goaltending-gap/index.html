Last Season's Save Percentage Gap Ran New Jersey's Way

Team save percentage and league rank, 2025-26

Start with the number the URL promised. Last season the Devils finished with a .893 team save percentage and the Senators finished with a .882 team save percentage. Neither figure is flattering on its own, but the ranks show how far apart they sat: New Jersey was 19th of 32 in team save percentage in 2025-26, while Ottawa was 31st, next to last in the league.

That's the whole case for New Jersey in one line, and it's worth being precise about what it covers. The .893 save percentage last season belongs to the entire Devils team, every goalie who dressed, not to any one netminder. Jake Allen is listed to start for New Jersey and Linus Ullmark is listed to start for Ottawa, and nothing on this page measures either of them on his own. What it does measure is that over the 2025-26 season, pucks aimed at the Ottawa net found their way in more often than pucks aimed at the New Jersey net.

Last Season the Better Save Percentage Did Not Mean Fewer Goals Against

Goals allowed per game, 2025-26

Here's the wrinkle that keeps this from being a clean goaltending story. Despite the better team save percentage, .893 to .882, the Devils allowed 3.1 goals a game last season and the Senators allowed 3. New Jersey ranked 18th of 32 in goals allowed per game in 2025-26, and Ottawa ranked 13th. The club with the weaker save percentage kept more goals off the board.

Save percentage is a rate; goals against is the result. Last season Ottawa got the better result out of the worse rate, which tells you the gap in goal, real as it was, isn't the entire defensive picture. For New Jersey it means the .893 team save percentage last season didn't buy a goals against number in the top half of the league. The Devils need more from their crease edge than it delivered in 2025-26.

Last Season Ottawa Finished Its Chances Far More Often

Shooting percentage and scoring, 2025-26

The goaltending gap has a mirror image at the other end, and it runs hard the other way. Ottawa posted a shooting percentage of 11.6 last season, 10th of 32, while New Jersey posted a shooting percentage of 9.3, 31st. That's the same next to last slot Ottawa held in team save percentage, just on the opposite side of the puck. The goals followed: the Senators scored 3.39 a game in 2025-26, 8th in the league, and the Devils scored 2.8, 27th.

This is why the save percentage edge alone doesn't settle anything. Save percentage measures how often your own net gets beaten; shooting percentage measures how often you beat the other one. Last season Ottawa held the far bigger edge in the second, and New Jersey held the smaller edge in the first. Against a club that scored 3.39 goals a game last season, a .893 team save percentage has less cushion than it looks.

The mirror

Last season Ottawa ranked 10th in shooting percentage; New Jersey ranked 31st.

Last Season's Penalty Kill Is Where Ottawa's Crease Showed

Power play and penalty kill, 2025-26

If you want to see where a .882 team save percentage hurt Ottawa last season, the penalty kill is the obvious place to look. The Senators killed 75.8 percent of penalties in 2025-26, 29th of 32. The Devils killed 79.3 percent, 17th. A shorthanded unit leans on its goaltending as much as anything on the ice, and Ottawa's kill sat right where its save percentage did, near the bottom.

The power play cuts the other way. Ottawa converted at 24 percent last season, 8th of 32, and New Jersey at 22 percent, 13th. Going by last season's tables, every Devils power play here pits a unit ranked 13th against a kill ranked 29th, which is the most direct route New Jersey has to exploit Ottawa's crease. Every Ottawa power play pits a unit ranked 8th against a kill ranked 17th.

The opening

Last season Ottawa's penalty kill ranked 29th of 32. That's New Jersey's door.

Last Season's Margin Is What New Jersey Has to Overcome

Goal differential and standings, 2025-26

Strip it back to the scoreboard and Ottawa was simply the better team last season. The Senators finished at +0.39 goals a game, 8th of 32, for a goal differential of +32. The Devils finished at -0.3 goals a game, 24th, for a goal differential of -24. That's the hole the save percentage edge has to fill.

The standings agreed. Ottawa went 44-27-11 for 99 standings points in 2025-26, and New Jersey went 42-37-3 for 87. Even shutouts, where you'd expect the better team save percentage to show up, leaned Ottawa last season, 4 to 2. The Devils were better in goal and still lost the margin.

The margin

Last season Ottawa was +0.39 goals a game. New Jersey was -0.3.

Where Both Clubs Stand Now, and What Last Season Says About This One

2026-27 records and 2025-26 splits

The new season is young enough that it mostly echoes the old one. Ottawa is 4-1-0 in 2026-27 and New Jersey is 2-2-0. Last season the Devils went 21-17-3 at Prudential Center and the Senators went 21-15-5 on the road, so home ice didn't change the shape of this matchup in 2025-26. Both closed last season in decent form, Ottawa at 6-3-1 over its last ten and New Jersey at 5-4-1.

So the goaltending gap is real, and it's the best argument New Jersey has. Allen is listed to start for the Devils and Ullmark is listed to start for the Senators, and if Ottawa's crease looks anything like last season's .882 team save percentage, New Jersey will get its looks. The trouble is that the Devils' shooting percentage of 9.3 last season ranked 31st, which makes them the club in this game least equipped to punish a leaky net. Last season's numbers say the save percentage edge narrows this game; they don't say it flips it.

The bottom line

Last season New Jersey had the better crease. Ottawa had the better shooters, and that won out.
