How each engine works

MLBBaseball Simulator

Plays the game out one plate appearance at a time. Every batter faces the selected starter with real outcome probabilities, runners advance through a base-and-out state machine, and the box score is what those events produced.

  • Starter and bullpen matter. Choose the starting pitchers and watch the same two clubs move.
  • Real rate stats, batter against pitcher, combined through the odds-ratio method.
  • Run distribution and win probability across as many simulations as you want to run.
  • Current, classic and mixed-era matchups, plus a separate season and playoff simulator.
How accurate it is

Scored out of sample on the 2,434 games of a season the model never saw, each projected from the season before it. The home side won 54.3% of them, which is the bar any model has to clear. The engine's own score is not published here yet, and it will be a TEAM-level number when it is: the scoring run uses league-average lineups, so it says nothing about individual batters or announced starters.

NFLFootball Simulator

Simulates any matchup from current projected rosters and player usage, so the quarterback on the field and the way touches and targets are shared reflect this season's depth chart rather than last season's.

  • Quarterback impact measured as a change from the team's baseline, so a backup starting moves the score.
  • Opponent-adjusted strength, offence and defence solved jointly against schedule quality.
  • Special teams included: field-goal accuracy by distance, punting and return threat.
  • Venue and kickoff window, plus pace, drives per game, turnovers and defensive scores.
How accurate it is

Held to the same discipline in code: training, calibration and test seasons are kept disjoint, and the test season is scored once so nothing in it can tune the model. In a backtest on 285 held-out 2025 games (the model re-run with only pre-kickoff data, not a grade of live predictions), it called the winner correctly 63.86% of the time, compared with 53.33% for always selecting the home team, with calibration error of approximately 3.3%. The closing betting market was more accurate, and the model does not beat it. Single-game margins remain highly variable.

NBABasketball Simulator

Runs the game as possessions. Pace decides how many there are, efficiency and shot profile decide what happens in them, and who takes the shot is weighted by real shot volume among the five players on the floor.

  • Offensive rebounds are contested, this offence's rate against that defence's, and a board continues the possession.
  • Fouls and foul trouble, with a sixth foul removing a player for the rest of the game.
  • Endgame and overtime played under real closing rules rather than extrapolated.
  • Home court worth roughly two and a half to three points, removable for a neutral floor.
How accurate it is

Scored on 3,701 games across four seasons, each projected only from games played before it: winner called 66.7% (home team alone: 54.8%), calibration error 1.9%.

NHLHockey Simulator

Built around shots and goaltending. Shot volume is drawn with the spread real shot counts carry, and the starting goaltender's save percentage bends the opposing goal rate directly rather than decorating the result.

  • Pick the starter, or leave the projected goaltenders in place and change one to see the swing.
  • Special teams both ways, with penalty minutes that always equal twice the other team's power plays.
  • Goals and assists dealt to named skaters from their real per-sixty rates and ice time.
  • Score effects, overtime, the shootout and the empty net all handled explicitly.
How accurate it is

Scored on 3,939 games across four seasons out of sample: winner called 57.6% (home team alone: 54.2%), calibration error 1.8%. A real edge, and a small one. Hockey is like that.

What separates these from a random score generator

Most free “sports simulators” are a random number with a team name attached. Pull the lever, get 27 to 24. Nothing inside them knows who is playing, and running the same matchup a thousand times tells you nothing you did not already know. These four are built the other way round.

The game is played, not guessed

Each engine resolves the unit its sport is actually made of, whether that is a plate appearance, a drive, a possession or a shot, and the score is the sum of what happened. That is why the box score reconciles: every team number is the sum of the player lines, and the runs, points or goals add up to the final score, because they came out of the same simulated events.

Team strength is solved, not read off a table

Points scored and points allowed do not know who a team played, and a soft schedule flatters them. The NBA, NHL and NFL engines solve every team's offence and defence jointly from actual results, so a rating carries the quality of the opposition with it. In hockey that difference is most of the model: raw goals for and against are largely shooting and goaltending luck.

Current data, where the sport has it

The NFL simulator uses current projected rosters and player usage. The NBA simulator uses current rosters, rotations and minutes. The NHL simulator uses current rosters and real starting goaltenders. The MLB simulator lets you set the starting pitchers yourself and anchors run output to a model that already accounts for park, bullpen and era context.

One run is a night, many runs are a matchup

Set a simulation count and the win probability, the total and the distribution charts come from that whole batch rather than from one lucky game. One run shows you a plausible evening. A few thousand show you the shape of the matchup and how wide the realistic range really is, which is usually wider than people expect.

Sport specific logic, not one engine in four jerseys

Foul trouble matters in basketball and does not exist in baseball. A goaltender change moves a hockey projection more than almost any single input moves a football one. Each simulator models the things that decide games in its own sport, which is exactly why these stayed four separate products rather than becoming one page with a sport dropdown.

The accuracy number is published

Every simulator claims to be accurate. These were scored out of sample, each game projected using only results that came before it, and the numbers sit on the page next to the baseline they had to beat. Where the edge is small, it says so. That is the same standard TrustMyRecord holds handicappers to.

Sports simulator FAQ

What is a sports simulator?

A sports simulator plays a game out rather than guessing at its result. These engines resolve the events a real game is made of, the plate appearances, drives, possessions and shifts, one at a time, using each team's and each player's real rates. The score, the statistics and the box score are what those simulated events added up to, so the same matchup run twice gives two different games in the same way real sport does.

Which sports can I simulate?

MLB, NFL, NBA and NHL. Each sport has its own engine and its own page, because the things that decide a baseball game are not the things that decide a hockey game. The suite is built so further sports can be added without changing the four that already exist.

Are the sports simulators free?

Yes, all four are free. The NBA and NHL simulators run with no account at all. The MLB and NFL simulators ask you to create a free TrustMyRecord account before a run, and your matchup and settings are carried through the sign-up so the simulation fires the moment you land back on the page.

How accurate are the simulators?

Two of the four carry a published number today. The NBA model called the winner 66.7% of the time across 3,701 games with a 1.9% calibration error, and the NHL model 57.6% across 3,939 games with a 1.8% calibration error, every game in both projected using only results that came before it. The MLB engine is scored against the 2,434 games of a season it never saw, where the home side won 54.3%, and the NFL engine against a test season kept disjoint from its training and calibration seasons in code. Neither of those two has its own figure published here yet, and an unverified number is worth less than saying so. Every number here is research, not a betting edge, and none of it is betting advice.

How many times can I run the same matchup?

As many as you like. Each simulator takes a simulation count, and the win probability, the total and the distribution charts come from that whole batch rather than from one lucky game. One run shows you a plausible night; a few thousand show you the shape of the matchup.
