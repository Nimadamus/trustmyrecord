What the numbers are worth

The analysis

The section above measures these two clubs. This one prices what the measurements are worth: the possession a third down edge buys, the points a red zone rate is really carrying, and where the simulation and the board disagree.

Efficiency

Where the drives actually end

Third down is the down that decides whether a drive keeps going or hands the ball back, and San Francisco is converting 63.2% of them against 34.6% for Arizona. These two are seeing about 11 third downs a game, so that edge is worth roughly 3.2 extra sets of downs to San Francisco, which is a drive that lives instead of a punt.

Inside the 20 the difference between a touchdown and a field goal is four points every single trip, and San Francisco is finishing 87.5% of its red zone trips with a touchdown against 50.0% for Arizona. Three trips a game at that gap is most of a score, and none of it shows up in a yardage column.

Turnovers move scores faster than anything else on the page: Arizona is +2 on the season against +1 for San Francisco. An extra possession is worth about two points of expectation on its own, and it is also the least stable number here, so it is a reason to respect a side rather than to price one.

Variance

What actually beats the number

48% of the ten thousand runs finish inside one score and 11% land on exactly three points, which is the most common margin in the sport and the reason three and seven are priced differently from four and eight.

The board is laying 8.5 with San Francisco, so there is no push to hide behind. Every one of those one score finishes settles the ticket one way or the other.

The other tail is real as well. 30% of the runs are decided by fourteen or more, so this is not a game that only knows how to be close.
