What the numbers are worth

The tables above measure these two clubs. This reads them: each offence crossed with the staff it is facing, the bullpen gap in runs rather than in ERA, and what the posted price is charging over the fair one.

Cross each offence with the staff it is actually facing and you get a rough run environment for this game: Blue Jays score 4.03 a game against a staff giving up 4.63, which splits to 4.33, and Orioles score 4.44 against 4.30 allowed, which splits to 4.37. Put together that is about 8.7 runs. The board is at 7.5, so the season rates sit 1.2 runs above the number. That is a matchup neutral estimate and nothing more: it carries no park, no starting pitcher and no weather, all three of which the book has already priced. Read it as the baseline the adjustments are moving away from, not as a number to bet into.

On the season Orioles carries the better bat, 0.709 OPS against 0.692. The shape of it matters as much as the size: isolated power runs 0.134 for Blue Jays and 0.161 for Orioles, and the club with the power is the one that can score without stringing three hits together, which is the only way most teams score against a good bullpen.

The two moneyline prices add up to 104.1% rather than 100, and that 4.1 points is the book's margin, charged whichever side you take. Take it out and Orioles is a 54.3% side. The ticket itself still needs 56.5% to break even at -130, so the difference between the fair number and the break even is what the price costs you before the game starts.
