What the numbers are worth

The tables above measure these two clubs. This reads them: each offence crossed with the staff it is facing, the bullpen gap in runs rather than in ERA, and what the posted price is charging over the fair one.

Cross each offence with the staff it is actually facing and you get a rough run environment for this game: Athletics score 4.35 a game against a staff giving up 4.09, which splits to 4.22, and Rays score 4.56 against 5.70 allowed, which splits to 5.13. Put together that is about 9.3 runs. The board is at 8.0, so the season rates sit 1.3 runs above the number. That is a matchup neutral estimate and nothing more: it carries no park, no starting pitcher and no weather, all three of which the book has already priced. Read it as the baseline the adjustments are moving away from, not as a number to bet into.

The bullpens are the gap nobody prices until the sixth: Rays at 4.10 against 5.42 for Athletics. A modern pen throws about three innings a night, so that difference is worth roughly 0.44 runs a game to Rays, and it lands in exactly the innings where a one run lead is either protected or handed back.

On the season Rays carries the better bat, 0.735 OPS against 0.720. The shape of it matters as much as the size: isolated power runs 0.157 for Athletics and 0.147 for Rays, and the club with the power is the one that can score without stringing three hits together, which is the only way most teams score against a good bullpen. Those two point at different clubs here, so the better offence and the more dangerous one are not the same team tonight.

The two moneyline prices add up to 104.5% rather than 100, and that 4.5 points is the book's margin, charged whichever side you take. Take it out and Rays is a 62.3% side. The ticket itself still needs 65.2% to break even at -187, so the difference between the fair number and the break even is what the price costs you before the game starts.
